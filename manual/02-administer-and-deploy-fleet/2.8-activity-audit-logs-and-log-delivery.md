# Activity, audit logs, and log delivery

Fleet’s activity record helps administrators and security staff understand changes to the devices they manage. Delivering those events to your existing logging platform puts endpoint work alongside identity and infrastructure events, where investigators can search them together. This chapter explains how to choose that destination, configure delivery, and confirm the records arrive.

## Purpose and scope

[1.5 Audit and activity](../01-foundations/1.5-audit-and-activity.md) explains which administrative changes, requested work, automation, and device outcomes Fleet records. Here, you will configure delivery for its three outbound streams: activity audit events, osquery results, and osquery status. Each stream has its own settings and purpose.

[8.2](../08-troubleshooting/8.2-log-surfaces.md) inventories every log surface Fleet has, and [8.12](../08-troubleshooting/8.12-audit-logs.md) covers diagnosis when a record is missing or puzzling. This chapter is what those two point back at.

## What each Fleet tier includes

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Every deployment includes the activity record. Premium adds automatic delivery of eligible activity events to an external destination.

**The activity record itself is available on every Fleet deployment.** Free and Premium alike, readable in the UI and through the Activities API, though the *global* feed is readable only by global roles ([1.4](../01-foundations/1.4-identity-and-roles.md)). Every Fleet deployment can answer who changed what and when, for the changes Fleet enumerates and for the people it can attribute; some rows record an observed outcome and carry no actor at all.

Streaming eligible activity records to an external destination requires Fleet Premium.

On Fleet Free, administrators can still review the history in the interface and retrieve it through the Activities API.

## Three streams, three switches

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Configure each stream you need. Choosing an osquery-results destination leaves the status and activity-audit destinations unchanged.

| Stream | What it carries | Setting | Default | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Activity audit** | The administrative and observed-event record this chapter is mostly about | `activity_enable_audit_log`, then `activity_audit_log_plugin` | Off; plugin `filesystem` | **Premium** |
| **osquery results** | The rows your scheduled reports produce on hosts | `osquery_result_log_plugin` | `filesystem` | Free and Premium |
| **osquery status** | osquery's own health messages, forwarded through Fleet | `osquery_status_log_plugin` | `filesystem` | Free and Premium |

The two osquery plugins take the same set of destinations as each other: `filesystem`, `firehose`, `kinesis`, `lambda`, `pubsub`, `kafkarest`, `nats`, `splunk`, `webhook` and `stdout`. They are set independently, so results and status can go to different places, and they are **on by default** in the sense that they always write somewhere; what you are choosing is where, not whether.

The activity audit stream is disabled by default, requires Premium, and uses its own plugin setting. The delivery procedure below applies to that stream.

For scheduled reports, also enable automations on each report whose results you want forwarded ([4.2](../04-know-your-devices/4.2-run-queries-and-reports.md)). New reports have automations off by default. Two cases bypass this per-report filter: when stored reporting is disabled server-wide, and when incoming results belong to reports Fleet does not recognize, such as reports configured in osquery outside Fleet.

## Choosing a destination

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Most organizations reach for external delivery for one of three reasons, and they lead to different answers.

**Retention** is the simplest: Fleet's own activity history is subject to the expiry settings covered in [2.7](2.7-organization-and-server-settings.md), and a compliance obligation may require keeping records longer than you want to keep them in Fleet's database. Delivery to durable storage is most of the answer, though not all of it: **host-only activities never enter the stream**, so a destination is not a complete archive of what Fleet held ([1.5](../01-foundations/1.5-audit-and-activity.md)).

**Correlation** helps explain a change in context. A Fleet event beside identity and infrastructure events from the same period can show how an endpoint action fits into a wider investigation. Use the logging platform your investigators already search when that is the goal.

**Access control** lets security staff investigate without needing a Fleet account. Deliver events to a system where they already have appropriate access, and manage retention and permissions there.

Decide which of these you are solving for, because it determines who should own the destination, who reads it, and how long it is kept. Settle those three before enabling anything.

## Turn on delivery

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Two server settings control this, and the second only matters once the first is on.

`activity_enable_audit_log` turns delivery on. It defaults to off. As an environment variable it is `FLEET_ACTIVITY_ENABLE_AUDIT_LOG`.

`activity_audit_log_plugin` chooses where events go. It defaults to `filesystem`. As an environment variable it is `FLEET_ACTIVITY_AUDIT_LOG_PLUGIN`.

In config-file form:

```yaml
activity:
  enable_audit_log: true
  audit_log_plugin: firehose
```

The available plugins at Fleet 4.90.0 are `filesystem`, `firehose`, `kinesis`, `lambda`, `pubsub`, `kafkarest`, `nats`, `splunk`, and `stdout`. Each has its own configuration except `stdout`, which needs none. Choose based on what your organization already operates rather than on what looks tidiest; the destination someone already monitors beats the destination that is technically neater.

Configure the selected plugin’s destination settings as part of the same change. Every plugin except `stdout` requires additional configuration, and Fleet can start even when that configuration is incomplete.

The first delivery run includes the eligible unstreamed history already in the database, oldest first. Fleet marks records as streamed after delivery, so enabling the feature on an established deployment can produce a substantial initial backlog. Allow capacity for that first run. Host-only activities remain excluded from both the backlog and subsequent streaming.

For managed cloud hosting, ask Fleet to configure logging. On self-hosted deployments, the infrastructure owner changes these server settings using the process described in [2.7](2.7-organization-and-server-settings.md).

### Change the filesystem destination if you use it

Enabling audit logging without selecting a plugin uses `filesystem`, whose default destination is `/tmp/audit`.

That path has two properties that make it a poor permanent home: `/tmp` is cleared by the operating system, and it is local to a single Fleet instance, so a deployment running several instances writes several partial logs. Fine for confirming events are being produced, and worth replacing with either a real path your log forwarder watches or a plugin that ships events directly.

## Verification and ownership

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Make a low-risk, clearly identifiable change: rename a test label, or create and delete a placeholder. Something you can search for unambiguously.

Allow up to five minutes for the asynchronous delivery before checking the destination. An immediate search may run before Fleet has sent the event.

Once the event arrives, confirm four things about it rather than just its presence:

- The **actor** is the identity you expect, and is recognisable. This is where a well-named service identity from [2.6](2.6-user-accounts-roles-and-service-identities.md) pays off.
- The **scope** is as expected, remembering that an activity payload carries the fleet and the item but not the labels that were targeted or the hosts that resolved, so it tells you which fleet an action was aimed at rather than which hosts received it ([1.3](../01-foundations/1.3-hosts-fleets-labels.md)).
- The **timestamp** makes sense next to your other systems, which is what makes correlation work.
- The people who will use this during an investigation **can find it**, using the tools they would actually reach for at the time. An event that arrives in a store nobody can query is not evidence.

Run that final check with someone who would conduct the investigation, using their normal access and search tools.

Ongoing, three things need an owner: the destination itself, the retention period configured there, and monitoring that events are still arriving. A delivery pipeline that quietly stops looks exactly like a period of no administrative activity, which is why the monitoring matters more here than for most integrations.

## Edge cases and precedence

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) **Oversized events are dropped rather than truncated or retried.** Destinations batch events before sending, and an event too large for its destination's limit is discarded with a notice written to the Fleet server log. The limits differ by destination, so the threshold you care about depends on which one you chose.

If individual external records are missing while the activities remain visible in Fleet, check server logs for size-limit notices. A successful destination connection does not rule out oversized records being dropped.

**Delivery is asynchronous and can lag by up to five minutes**, which is covered above under verification but bears repeating here because it interacts with the previous point: an event that has not arrived may be late rather than lost, and the two look identical for the first few minutes.

Retry behavior depends on whether the write failed or the plugin reported success after discarding an oversized record.

Audit streaming tracks which activities it has already sent. When a write fails, Fleet stops at that activity, leaves it unmarked, and retries from there on the next run a few minutes later. So an outage delays delivery rather than losing it, and the backlog drains once the destination returns.

Several plugins discard an individual oversized record while reporting the batch as written. Fleet then marks the activity as streamed and does not retry it. Use the server-log notice to identify that gap; it will not repair itself when the destination becomes available.

The practical consequence is that a destination outage is an operational problem, while a size limit is a silent data-integrity one. Size the destination for your largest plausible activity record rather than your typical one.
