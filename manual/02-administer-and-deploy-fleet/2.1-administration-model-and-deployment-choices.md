# Administration model and deployment choices

Setting up Fleet begins with three decisions: who operates the service, who holds authority inside it, and which operating systems will be managed in Fleet.

**Operating the service** covers the Fleet server and everything underneath it: a database holding the record, a cache carrying work in progress, object storage for large files, a certificate the agents will verify, and the backups, monitoring and periodic upgrades that keep all of it running. Fleet can take that on for you (managed cloud), or you can run it yourself (self-hosted).

**Holding authority** covers who has an account, what each person is allowed to change, over which devices, and how they sign in.

**The OS relationships** are the certificates, tokens and enterprise bindings that let Fleet manage macOS, Windows or Android at all. Most of them live in Apple's, Microsoft's or Google's consoles rather than in Fleet, and several have scheduled expirations out of your control.

Two of the three may already be determined. Eligibility for managed cloud hosting depends on your license tier and the size of your estate, and the identity provider is an organization-level choice rather than a Fleet one. What is left to decide is how authority is divided and who is accountable for each OS relationship.

Four of the choices in this chapter are difficult to reverse once devices are enrolled. They are collected at the end so you can settle them before you start.

## Purpose and scope

This chapter covers those three decisions, how to record who owns the result, and how to prove the arrangement works on a small population before the whole estate depends on it.

The mechanics come later. Connecting an identity provider is [2.2](2.2-identity-providers-sso-scim-and-role-sync.md). Setting up MDM is [2.6](2.6-mdm-architecture-and-foundations.md). Sizing and running the infrastructure yourself is [2.9](2.9-self-hosting-architecture-and-capacity.md).

## Where Fleet runs

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) There are two ways to run Fleet, and eligibility decides which of them are open to you.

**Fleet offers managed cloud hosting to Fleet Premium customers with large deployments.** Fleet runs the service and the infrastructure underneath it, and your team works on organization settings, identities, platform connections, and device operations. As of Fleet 4.90.1 the documented threshold for fully managed hosting is deployments **larger than 700 hosts**.

**Self-hosting is the path for everyone else, and it is a well-trodden one.** You run the Fleet server, MySQL, Redis, TLS, object storage where you use it, networking, backups, and monitoring. Fleet documents deployments on AWS with Terraform, Kubernetes, GCP, Render, and Docker Compose, and Fleet Premium is available self-hosted with support.

**Premium capabilities are identical on both paths.** What changes is who carries the pager for MySQL.

If you are self-hosting, [1.6 The Fleet server](../01-foundations/1.6-the-fleet-server.md) explains why the state is split across three stores, which is the model that makes the capacity and backup decisions in [2.9](2.9-self-hosting-architecture-and-capacity.md) make sense.

## Fleet administrators and infrastructure owners

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Both paths split responsibility along the same line, and naming it early saves an argument during the first incident that lands on it.

**Fleet administrators own** identity provider configuration, roles and their scopes, fleet structure, and the platform management connections to Apple, Microsoft and Google.

**Infrastructure owners own** service availability, the three state stores, backups and their restoration, monitoring, and upgrades.

**Both own the boundary**: who gets called when the other side is implicated, and who approves a change that crosses it.

On managed cloud hosting the second of those belongs to Fleet, which is the practical difference between the two paths. [2.12](2.12-production-readiness-and-handoff.md) turns this split into a handover document.

## Define the administration boundary

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Fleet's access model gives you two levels to work with, and the useful discipline is to decide which decisions belong at each one before you create a single account.

A small **global administrator** group should own the settings that affect everyone: identity-provider configuration, fleet creation, MDM service connections, server settings, audit-log delivery, and access review. These are the settings where one change reaches every device, and they deserve a group small enough that its members can talk to each other.

**Fleet-level roles** then delegate the day-to-day work to the teams that own particular device populations. A computer lab, a business unit, or a regional support group can run their own devices without reaching anyone else's.

The reasoning behind that split, and what actually decides whether a request is allowed, are covered in [1.4 Identity and roles](../01-foundations/1.4-identity-and-roles.md). This chapter is about writing the answer down.

## Record who owns what, before you configure anything

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Build a deployment ownership record now and keep it somewhere both teams can reach. Each row needs a named person rather than a team, a named backup, how often it is reviewed, and where the record itself lives.

| What | Primary owner | Backup owner | Review cadence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Global settings | | | Quarterly |
| Each initial fleet | | | Quarterly |
| Identity provider connection | | | Quarterly, with access review |
| Each OS management connection | | | Monthly, against renewal dates |
| Service infrastructure, when self-hosting | | | Quarterly |
| Backup and restore | | | Per restore test |
| Escalation path | | | Quarterly |

Name individuals rather than teams. A team has no calendar and cannot be paged.

OS management connections need the tightest cadence, because several expire on schedules set by Apple or Microsoft rather than by you. [2.6](2.6-mdm-architecture-and-foundations.md) covers what expires and when.

## Use a pilot as the first production test

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Choose a pilot fleet with clear ownership and people who can tell you when something feels wrong from the end-user side. Treat it as the first production deployment, run somewhere the consequences are small.

Before expanding, confirm the whole chain works there: sign-in through your identity provider, roles landing as intended, activity appearing in the audit record, MDM connected, devices enrolling, and at least one representative management action reaching a device and reporting back.

Enrollment proves only that a device can be reached. Whether work sent to it arrives, executes and reports a result is a separate chain with its own failure modes. Send a profile or run a script, and watch it complete.

## Verification and ownership

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) You are ready to expand when the pilot answers yes to all of these:

- An administrator signs in through the identity provider and lands with the role you expected, in the scope you expected.
- A person removed from the relevant group in your identity provider loses Fleet access.
- Every action taken during the pilot appears in the activity record with the right actor.
- Each MDM connection is live, and you know the date each one needs renewing.
- A representative management action reached a device and reported a result.
- Every row in the ownership record has a named primary and backup, and both know it.

Ongoing, the global administrator group owns the access review and the renewal calendar. Fleet owners own their own device populations. Whoever owns the infrastructure owns the restore test, which is worth running before you need it rather than during.

## Decisions to settle before enrollment

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Most of these choices can be revisited later at moderate cost. Four are commitments, and settling them before the first device enrolls costs nothing now.

**The server URL.** Changing it after Apple devices have MDM turned on requires an end user to turn MDM off and back on, per device, by hand. On Android it cannot be changed at all once management is connected, because device events are delivered to one fixed address. [2.4](2.4-organization-and-server-settings.md) covers what else it reaches.

**Whether you can use managed cloud hosting at all.** It requires Fleet Premium and a deployment above Fleet's stated size threshold, so for smaller estates the decision is already made and the plan should assume self-hosting.

**Your database topology, when self-hosting.** Fleet supports one writable primary with read replicas, and does not support multi-writer or group-replication clusters. If your organization's platform standard is a multi-writer cluster, resolve it before deployment. [2.9](2.9-self-hosting-architecture-and-capacity.md) covers the constraint and what it means for availability design. On managed cloud hosting, Fleet has already made this choice.

**Which identity provider owns access.** Retrofitting single sign-on onto a deployment where accounts were created by hand means reconciling two sets of identities. Connecting it first means there is only ever one.

Everything else in the setup sequence tolerates iteration. These four stop being decisions the moment the first device enrolls.
