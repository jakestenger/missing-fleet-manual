# How to use this manual

This aims to be a complete guide to running Fleet. It is written for the people who operate it: endpoint administrators, infrastructure administrators, security engineers, IT teams, and anyone working alongside them. Completeness here is audited rather than assumed: the [capability index](../09-appendices/a.1-capability-index.md#where-this-index-ends) closes with the live register of anything a reader might search for that no chapter yet owns, and that register shrinks as the remaining chapters are written. See [Audience, prerequisites, scope, and non-goals](#audience-prerequisites-scope-and-non-goals) below for exactly what "complete" covers and what it deliberately does not.

It is built to be read two ways. Front to back, it explains how Fleet is designed to work and why, for anyone new to Fleet or planning a rollout or a migration to it. Opened to a single page, it gives you exact settings, commands, platform limitations, or explanations of why something failed, which is what you want for reference or troubleshooting.

## Audience, prerequisites, scope, and non-goals

This manual is for endpoint administrators, infrastructure administrators, security engineers, and IT teams who operate Fleet, not for people building Fleet itself or extending it with new code. It assumes you already know your own fleet of devices and your organization's policies; it does not teach general device-management concepts, MDM protocols, or osquery's SQL dialect from first principles. It also does not reproduce a `fleetctl` command's full flag list: that is read from the client you have installed with `fleetctl <command> --help`, the only listing guaranteed to match your binary, while [a.7](../09-appendices/a.7-fleetctl-command-reference.md) carries each command's contract and the results that mislead instead. The deployment chapters are scoped the same way: [2.2](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.2-self-hosting-architecture-and-capacity.md), [2.3](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.3-deploy-on-aws-or-gcp.md), and [2.4](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.4-deploy-with-containers-or-virtual-machines.md) teach the self-hosting model and the reference paths well enough to operate, not every possible topology, and they point at Fleet's reference architectures where a specific build belongs there. Where a workflow depends on something Fleet does not own (a vendor's own console, a certificate authority, an identity provider), this manual explains the Fleet side of the integration and names what you still have to bring.

It targets Fleet 4.90.1, self-hosted or managed cloud, and it is explanation and reference, not a guided labs course: there are no exercises with answer keys. [1.1](../01-foundations/1.1-what-fleet-is.md#try-fleet-without-deploying-anything) covers `fleetctl preview`, the closest thing to a sandbox, so you can try a workflow against a real (if disposable) deployment before trusting it against production.

"Complete" is audited, not assumed, and the audit is public: read [the capability index's closing register](../09-appendices/a.1-capability-index.md#where-this-index-ends) for outcomes with no owning chapter yet, and [the command reference's owning-chapter list](../09-appendices/a.7-fleetctl-command-reference.md#which-commands-have-an-owning-chapter) for `fleetctl` commands in the same position. Both are live documents that shrink as gaps close. Check them rather than assuming either this page or your memory of a past read is current.

## Where to start

Pick the path that matches where you already are.

**If you are evaluating Fleet or deploying it for the first time:**

- Read Part I in full, [What Fleet is](../01-foundations/1.1-what-fleet-is.md) through [The Fleet server](../01-foundations/1.6-the-fleet-server.md), then [2.1](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.1-administration-model-and-deployment-choices.md) to choose between managed cloud hosting and self-hosting. The route branches on that choice:
  - **Managed Fleet:** the self-hosting chapters do not apply to you. Go straight to [2.5](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.5-identity-providers-sso-scim-and-role-sync.md) and continue through administration and platform configuration.
  - **Self-hosted:** start with [2.2](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.2-self-hosting-architecture-and-capacity.md) for sizing, then take [2.3](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.3-deploy-on-aws-or-gcp.md) for the AWS or GCP reference architectures or [2.4](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.4-deploy-with-containers-or-virtual-machines.md) for containers or plain virtual machines on any cloud or none, whichever fits, then rejoin at [2.5](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.5-identity-providers-sso-scim-and-role-sync.md).
- Either way, Part III then gets devices connected. That is the path from nothing to a working deployment, not yet a production-ready or handed-off one. Before it carries real hosts under someone else's ownership, come back for [7.7](../07-operate-fleet/7.7-production-readiness-checklist-and-handoff.md), the production-readiness checklist and handoff. It assumes Part II and the rest of Part VII behind you, so treat it as the gate you clear once you have operated the deployment long enough to reach it, not a step to take right after Part III.

**If you are designing fleets, labels, or administration boundaries:**

- Start with [Hosts, fleets, labels, and targeting](../01-foundations/1.3-hosts-fleets-labels.md). These are the decisions that are cheap now and awkward later, so they repay the time. Follow with the Part II administration chapters, then Part VI when you want it all in version control.

**If you already run an estate and want to get more from it**:

- Part IV covers reading device state and Part V covers changing it. Those two parts are the day-to-day of Fleet.

**If you are automating:**

- Part VI covers configuration-as-code with GitOps, direct automation through the REST API, Fleet's `fleetctl` command-line client, outbound integrations, and connecting an AI assistant through Fleet's MCP server ([6.6](../06-automate-fleet/6.6-connect-fleet-to-an-ai-assistant.md)). Each feature chapter also has its own automation notes for the specifics.

**If you are administering the service itself:**

- Start with [The Fleet server](../01-foundations/1.6-the-fleet-server.md) for the architecture, then Part VII for backups, upgrades, availability, and monitoring.

**If something is broken right now:**

- Go straight to Part VIII and begin with the diagnostic method. It is written to narrow a vague symptom down to one failing channel before you start reading logs, which is faster than it sounds.

## Four kinds of material, and how to spot them

Every feature chapter carries four kinds of information:

![](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) **Explanation** tells you why Fleet behaves the way it does. It is at the opening of a chapter, along with the diagrams.

![](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) **How-to** tells you how to carry out a task. Prerequisites, steps, and how to confirm it worked.

![](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) **Reference** tells you what a specific setting, field, command, or status means. Exact values, precedence rules, and limits. Most of it lives in the appendices, linked to from the chapter.

![](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting.svg) **Troubleshooting** tells you why the result you expected did not arrive. Each chapter carries a short pointer into Part VIII, which owns the diagnostic material.

## Indexes

Fleet is easier to work with when you can start from the question you actually have rather than the one the table of contents expects. Six indexes will each get you to the same canonical page from a different direction.

If you know **the outcome you want**, such as installing software or rotating a recovery key, the [capability index](../09-appendices/a.1-capability-index.md) maps outcomes to chapters.

If you know **the device type** and want to know what is possible on it, the [platform capability matrix](../09-appendices/a.2-platform-capability-matrix.md) answers questions like what Fleet can do on Android, per platform, with the licence and prerequisites beside each answer. [1.1](../01-foundations/1.1-what-fleet-is.md) has the overview, and the chapter that owns a capability explains how it works.

If you know **the tool you are working in**, the [interface index](../09-appendices/a.5-interface-index.md) tells you whether something is reachable through GitOps, the API, fleetctl, or only the UI.

If you know **the symptom**, start at [8.1](../08-troubleshooting/8.1-diagnostic-method.md): it narrows a vague symptom down to one failing surface before handing you off. The rest of Part VIII is organised by surface, technique, and platform rather than by symptom.

If you know **the name of a thing**, a concept or an acronym such as APNs, DDM, or the node key, the [subject index](../09-appendices/a.10-subject-index.md) is an A-Z that points at where the book defines it.

If you are **connecting an AI assistant** and want to know what it can reach, the [Fleet MCP tool reference](../09-appendices/a.11-mcp-tool-reference.md) lists the fixed tool set and its arguments ([6.6](../06-automate-fleet/6.6-connect-fleet-to-an-ai-assistant.md) covers setting the server up).

## Why each chapter names a Fleet release

Every chapter names the Fleet release its behaviour was checked against, in its `verified_against` frontmatter. That is not a claim of uniform review maturity: every chapter also carries `status: drafting`, and only a minority carry a `reviewed_by` name yet. Naming a release records what the content was written and checked against; it does not by itself mean a second reviewer has confirmed it. Behaviour changes between releases, particularly API fields, GitOps YAML, fleetd update mechanics, operating system support, and which capabilities are in which plan.

**Find your own server's release before comparing it against a chapter's.** `GET /api/_version_/fleet/version`, authenticated with a session or an API token ([a.8](../09-appendices/a.8-api-action-and-endpoint-reference.md)), returns a `version` field with the exact release your server is running.

When you run another release, treat commands, fields, defaults, and edition gates as version-sensitive. Where a chapter carries its own **Version notes** section, read it. Most chapters don't carry one: that records the absence of a change worth flagging since the chapter's own `verified_against` release, not a guarantee that nothing changed; when your release differs from the one named, treat the gap as something to verify rather than something the book has already ruled out.

A chapter that says it is unverified means exactly that, and is worth more caution than one marked verified.

When updating the manual for a new release:

1. Review the release notes and the relevant source changes for every affected feature.
2. Update the chapter's `verified_against`, `verified_on`, and `verified_source`.
3. Record a version boundary wherever a reader on an older release needs different guidance.
4. Leave the explanation alone where the product model did not actually change.

## Conventions

- **Fleet** capitalised is the product or the company. **fleet** lowercase is the scoping construct that older material and parts of the API still call a team. **Fleet** capitalised at the beginning of a sentence could be either. In those cases, clarifying information will be provided.
- A **report** is the saved, runnable osquery query object you create in Fleet; running one ad hoc is a **live report**. Fleet renamed these from "query" and "live query" in release 4.82.0. osquery, the database, and Redis kept the older word, so **query** still appears throughout Part VIII where it correctly names osquery's mechanism rather than Fleet's object. See [a.6](../09-appendices/a.6-glossary-and-release-compatibility.md).
- Commands, API fields, YAML keys, filenames, and configuration values appear in `code formatting`.
- Platform differences are called out where they change a decision you have to make, not everywhere they exist.
- Links into Part VIII are deliberate handoffs to diagnostic material. They are not a substitute for the explanation, which stays in the feature chapter.
