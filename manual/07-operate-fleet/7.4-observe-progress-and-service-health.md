# Observe progress, performance, and service health

**A healthy process is not a healthy service.** Fleet's health check can be green while agents have stopped reporting, scheduled work has stopped running, and your audit log has stopped arriving somewhere.

This chapter is about closing that gap. It covers what Fleet emits, how to collect it, what it does not cover, and the part most easily skipped: **how to tell whether the signals you built are actually working.**

Monitoring platforms are yours. Dashboards, alert routing and paging policy are not Fleet's business and not this manual's.

## Measure service progress, not only uptime

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Several things can be separately unhealthy, and one check does not see any of them.

The outcomes worth tracking separately, because they fail independently:

| Outcome | Failing looks like |
|---|---|
| **Administrative access** | The interface and API are slow or erroring |
| **Agent reporting** | Hosts stop checking in; inventory ages |
| **Device management delivery** | Commands and profiles queue without landing |
| **Scheduled processing** | Vulnerability, app and maintenance jobs stop advancing |
| **Data freshness** | Something is still reporting, but what it reports is old |

> **Every one of those can fail while the health check stays green.** It tests the database and Redis. That is the whole of its coverage, and it is not a service check.

## Collect Fleet-native telemetry

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Two pipelines, unequal coverage, and one that is missing by default.

**The metrics endpoint carries request-level information**: HTTP families and a service-layer family covering a subset of administrative methods, plus runtime defaults. **It carries no business metric.** Nothing about hosts, policies, queues or freshness.

> **It is not mounted by default.** With no credentials configured and no explicit opt-out, the route is simply absent, and an ordinary request falls through to the web interface. That is not an error and not a refusal. **If you have never configured it, you do not have it**, and a check that expects a failure will not see one.

**The export pipeline carries traces and metrics**, including internal detail the metrics endpoint does not have: database pool statistics, error counters, cache counters. **The two overlap without either being a superset.** Decide what each is for rather than assuming one replaces the other.

> **Fleet's own instance identifier is regenerated on every start**, so it does not survive a restart and cannot be used to follow one node across a deployment. **Add a stable identity of your own**, from whatever your platform knows, or you will have signals you cannot attribute to a node over time.

## Know the coverage and sampling gaps

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Being precise about this is what makes the rest usable.

- **The health check covers the database and Redis. Not object storage**, which is not probed at startup either.
- **It does not name what failed.** Poll its individual checks separately if you want to know which one.
- **It does not test the server private key or whether anything encrypted is readable** ([7.3](7.3-back-up-restore-and-retire-service-state.md)).
- **Traces are sampled.** Totals derived from them are estimates.

### Three signals that report success when they should not

These matter more than the gaps, because a gap is silence and these are false reassurance:

> **A vulnerability freshness stamp returns the current time on an empty result set.** So "recently updated" and "found nothing at all, possibly because it never ran" look identical.
>
> **Two status endpoints return success even when the thing they report on failed.** Read the body, not the status ([6.3](../06-automate-fleet/6.3-use-the-fleet-rest-api.md)).
>
> **A latency metric named for microseconds is emitted in seconds.** A threshold set from the name will be wrong by six orders of magnitude, in the direction that never alerts.

## Monitor operational dependencies

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Where Fleet's signals stop and your vendor's start.

| Dependency | From Fleet | From the vendor |
|---|---|---|
| **MySQL** | Connection pool statistics, on the export pipeline only | Connections, replication lag, storage, CPU, slow queries |
| **Redis** | Very little | Memory, evictions, connections, replication |
| **Object storage** | **Nothing. Not even in the health check** | Latency, errors, availability |

**Object storage is the one to plan around.** Fleet will not tell you it is slow or gone; you find out when a package fails to install. **Watch it from the storage side.**

## Monitor queues, scheduled work, and feeds

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting.svg) The largest blind spot, and the workaround.

**Fleet has around three dozen possible scheduled jobs**, and which of them register depends on edition and configuration: a Free deployment registers substantially fewer than a Premium one. **Registration is not evidence of work.** The device-management schedules register whether or not device management is configured.

> **There is no supported way to read the schedule's own record of its runs.** No endpoint, no command. It is written to the database and no interface returns it.
>
> **And a run records completion even when every job inside it failed.** So even with database access, that record is not the signal you want.

**So monitor freshness instead of execution.** For each thing a schedule is supposed to advance, watch the timestamp of what it produced:

| What you care about | What to watch |
|---|---|
| Vulnerability processing | The freshness stamp, **with the empty-set caveat above** |
| Maintained-app and purchased-app refresh | Their own update times |
| Host vitals, policy results, software inventory | The per-host update times |
| Queued device work | Queue age, per host, from the host's own view |

**Age, not count.** A queue of a thousand that is draining is healthier than a queue of five that has not moved in a day ([7.2](7.2-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md) on the activated item that never completes).

## Monitor log and integration delivery

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting.svg) The failure most likely to lose something you needed.

Log destinations and webhooks are where Fleet hands data to something else, and both can fail quietly ([6.5](../06-automate-fleet/6.5-integrations-webhooks-and-external-workflows.md)).

> **Log-delivery failures are classified in a way that keeps them out of the places you would look.** They are treated as client errors, which means debug-level logging and exclusion from the error store and from exception reporting. **The failure most likely to cost you your audit trail is among the least likely to be noticed.**

**Count arrivals at the destination.** That is the only reliable evidence, and it is true of webhooks too. Everything on Fleet's side is partial.

## Establish baselines and leading capacity signals

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) What to record before you need it.

**Record a baseline before every change**, from whatever you can actually read: request latency and error rates, host counts and check-in freshness, queue ages, database and cache metrics from the vendor. [7.2](7.2-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md)'s last acceptance rung is meaningless without it.

> **Almost every leading indicator Fleet emits about its own resources is on the export pipeline only.** If you are running the metrics endpoint alone, you have request-level information and very little about pressure building inside the process. That is the practical reason to run both.

**Distinguish leading from lagging.** Latency rising, pool waits appearing and queue age growing are leading. Errors and timeouts are lagging: by then it has already happened. [7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md) acts on the leading ones.

## Define and test alerts

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) The section people skip, and the one that decides whether any of this works.

**Derive thresholds from [7.1](7.1-define-the-service-operating-model.md)'s objectives**, not from what a metric happens to do this week. If there is no objective, the alert has no meaning and will be tuned into silence.

**A small first set**, because six alerts that fire honestly beat sixty that nobody reads: administrative errors and latency, agent check-in freshness, device-work queue age, vulnerability freshness, log-delivery arrival, and the dependency signals your vendor provides.

> ### Prove the path end to end
>
> **A configured alert is not a working alert.** The one thing Fleet lets you exercise genuinely is the failing-policy notification path: resetting policy automations and then triggering the schedule produces a real delivery through the real channel, and it is **the only outbound channel that records both success and failure where an administrator can see them.**
>
> Use it to prove the whole chain: Fleet acts, the destination receives, and somebody is told. For everything else, test the alerting platform's own path and accept that you are testing your monitoring rather than Fleet.

**When a signal breaches without an explained change, that is [Part VIII](../08-troubleshooting/8.1-diagnostic-method.md).** This chapter's job is to notice; diagnosis is a different discipline and a different set of tools.
