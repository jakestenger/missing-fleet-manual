# Define the service operating model

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation-light.svg) Everything else in this part assumes decisions nobody has made yet. What is this service supposed to do, who owns which piece of it, what evidence is required before a change, and what happens when something is wrong at three in the morning.

**Most of this chapter is judgement rather than Fleet behaviour**, and it says so wherever that is true. What Fleet supplies is a set of raw materials of uneven quality, and the useful work is knowing which of your intentions can be measured and which cannot.

**A healthy process is not a healthy service.** That is the part's claim. This is the chapter where you decide what "healthy service" means for your deployment.

## Name the service outcomes

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation-light.svg) Several things can be separately broken, and one of them being fine tells you nothing about the others.

A workable division, and this grouping is a judgement rather than something Fleet defines:

| Outcome | The question it answers |
|---|---|
| **Administrative access** | Can people use Fleet? |
| **Agent reporting** | Are hosts still telling us about themselves? |
| **Device-management delivery** | Is work reaching devices? |
| **Scheduled processing** | Is Fleet's own background work advancing? |
| **Data freshness** | Is what we are reading current enough to act on? |

> **Fleet's health check tests the database and Redis, and nothing else.** That is its whole contract. **All five outcomes above can be failing while it is green**, which is why a service model is a different thing from a monitoring configuration ([7.4](7.4-observe-progress-and-service-health.md)).

**Name the outcomes before the indicators.** An indicator without an outcome behind it becomes an alert nobody can act on, and then an alert nobody reads.

## Set service levels and recovery objectives

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting-light.svg) The honest part: not everything you want to promise can be measured.

**Objectives are yours. Fleet publishes none**: no availability target, no recovery time, no data-loss target.

What matters when writing them is **what each measurement costs**, because that decides whether the objective survives contact with a real month:

| How hard | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Cheap and direct** | Request success and latency, if you are collecting Fleet's request metrics ([7.4](7.4-observe-progress-and-service-health.md)) |
| **Cheap but partial** | *Maximum* staleness **within a population you can already filter to**: one ordered query gives you the oldest and tells you nothing about how many are old |
| **Expensive** | The *share* of that population past an arbitrary age, which needs enumeration, because there is no staleness threshold to filter on |
| **Not available** | Whether a given scheduled job is currently up to date ([7.4](7.4-observe-progress-and-service-health.md) on why), and **per-node attribution of anything**, since Fleet's instance identifier does not survive a restart |

> **Write objectives against what you can actually measure**, and record the ones you would like and cannot as a known gap rather than as an aspiration. A target you cannot evaluate is not a target.

**Write each objective down with the same five things**, or it will not survive a handover:

| | |
|---|---|
| **The target** | The number, and the population it applies to |
| **How it is measured** | The specific source, and what it costs to read |
| **How planned maintenance is treated** | Excluded, budgeted, or counted. Decide once |
| **The owner** | Who is accountable when it is missed |
| **The gap** | Where your *measured* capability differs from the target |

**Recovery needs two numbers, not one**: how long recovery may take, and how much data may be lost. They are set separately and they trade off against each other.

Both come from [7.2](7.2-back-up-and-restore-service-state.md), and **the numbers you write should be the ones a rehearsal produced.** Where the declared target is better than the measured capability, that gap is the objective's most important field, because it is the thing to either fix or stop claiming.

## Assign operational ownership

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference-light.svg) The seams are where the arguments happen at three in the morning.

Fleet is one component among several, and each boundary needs an owner:

| Piece | Often owned by |
|---|---|
| Fleet itself | You |
| MySQL, Redis, object storage | A platform or database team |
| TLS certificates and name resolution | A platform team |
| Apple, Windows and Android provider relationships | You, sometimes with procurement |
| Identity provider | Somebody else |
| Observability platform | Somebody else |
| The Fleet MCP server, if you run one | You. It calls Fleet as an API-only identity you created and hold the bearer for; treat it as another consumer of your ownership, not a separate service someone else watches ([6.6](../06-automate-fleet/6.6-connect-fleet-to-an-ai-assistant.md)) |

> **One seam is sharper than it looks.** A read replica is normally described as a performance component and treated as optional. **If you have configured one and it becomes unavailable, Fleet's health check fails on every instance** ([7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md)), which is not what "optional" usually means.
>
> That makes database availability a **shared accountability** rather than somebody else's problem: the database team owns the component, you own the consequence, and both of you need to know the consequence exists before it happens.

**Write down who is called for each piece.** The exercise is short, and the alternative is discovering it during an incident.

## Build the operating calendar

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg) The dates Fleet knows, and the many it does not.

**Fleet surfaces a small number of dates.** Its licence expiry is readable through the configuration endpoint, and a few provider credentials show an expiry in the interface. **That is the short list.**

**Most of what expires does not announce itself.** Certificate and key material across the Apple, Windows and Android paths, integration credentials, and anything held in an external console all have deadlines that live outside Fleet and generate no warning inside it ([7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md)).

**So the calendar is a document you maintain**, and it needs the lead time each item requires rather than just its date. Renewing an Apple certificate is not a same-day task.

What belongs on it:

- **Every expiry**, with its lead time and its owner.
- **Release review**, at whatever cadence you chose ([7.3](7.3-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md)).
- **Access review** ([7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md)).
- **Recovery rehearsal** ([7.2](7.2-back-up-and-restore-service-state.md)).
- **Capacity review** ([7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md)).
- **Change windows**, if you use them.

## Define change gates and evidence

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg) What must be true before, and what must be shown after.

The gate has the same shape for any significant change: **a baseline, an acceptance criterion, an abort condition, and a named person who may invoke it.** [6.1](../06-automate-fleet/6.1-automation-design-and-change-control.md) asks for the same contract for automated changes, and the agreement between them is deliberate.

> ### There is no supported schema-isolated server canary
>
> The pattern people reach for is running the new version alongside the old for a slice of traffic. **Fleet provides no supported, schema-isolated way to do that.** One database schema serves the whole tier and the instances are interchangeable by design.
>
> The nearest equivalents are a **separate instance with its own database**, which tests the release but not your data, and a **rehearsal against restored production-shaped data** ([7.3](7.3-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md)), which tests your data but not your traffic.
>
> **Decide which of those you are doing.** What you cannot do is treat a rolling deployment as a schema-isolated canary, because every instance is talking to the same migrated schema regardless ([7.3](7.3-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md) on what a rolling replacement actually is).

Where the change is device-facing, **the canary is a group of devices rather than a group of servers**, and that does work: a pilot ring, a bounded fleet, a small label ([5.1](../05-manage-devices/5.1-plan-target-and-govern-device-changes.md)).

**Evidence, not confidence.** [7.3](7.3-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md)'s acceptance ladder is the model: a list of things checked in order, each one written down.

## Maintain the runbook portfolio

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference-light.svg) One runbook per continuity risk, each owned and each exercised.

The risks worth their own runbook come from the rest of this part, and they are not interchangeable:

**Write the ones your deployment actually needs.** Several below apply only if you have the thing they are about.

| Runbook | From | Only if |
|---|---|---|
| **Upgrade, and its recovery** | [7.3](7.3-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md) | Always |
| **Restore, including the isolation rules** | [7.2](7.2-back-up-and-restore-service-state.md) | Always |
| **Database primary failure and promotion** | [7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md) | Always |
| **Read-replica loss and degraded mode** | [7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md) | You run a replica |
| **Redis loss** | [7.2](7.2-back-up-and-restore-service-state.md) | Always |
| **Object storage unavailable** | [7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md) | You deliver software |
| **HTTPS certificate renewal** | [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md) | Always |
| **Apple certificate and token renewal** | [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md) | You manage Apple devices |
| **Windows enrolment certificate renewal** | [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md) | You manage Windows devices |
| **Credential compromise** | [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md) | Always |
| **Identity-provider outage and break-glass access** | [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md) | You use one. **Test that a local administrator can still sign in** |
| **Log delivery stopped** | [7.4](7.4-observe-progress-and-service-health.md) | You ship logs anywhere |
| **Regional recovery** | [7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md) | You have a second region |
| **Handoff to a new operator** | [7.7](7.7-production-readiness-checklist-and-handoff.md) | The service changes hands |
| **Retiring the deployment** | [7.8](7.8-retire-a-fleet-deployment.md) | Always |

**The Apple and Windows renewals are separate runbooks and not one MDM runbook**, because [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md) shows their consequences differ: one has a safe rollover path and the other does not.

**A runbook nobody has run is a document.** The restore and the certificate renewals are the two that most reward rehearsal, because both have steps that only reveal themselves when attempted.

**And a runbook has an owner**, or it has nobody.

---

An operating model is a set of decisions, and the value is in having made them rather than in the document that records them. **When something breaches without an explained change, that is [Part VIII](../08-troubleshooting/8.1-diagnostic-method.md).** This part is the planned half, and knowing which half you are in is itself one of the decisions.
