# Retire a Fleet deployment

**The operation nobody writes down until they need it.** Every other chapter in this part assumes the service has a future. This one is for the day it does not, and its job is to make sure the deployment ends by decision rather than by neglect.

Two steps lean on work other chapters define: the final backup is [7.2](7.2-back-up-and-restore-service-state.md)'s discipline, proven restorable and stored with its key material, and revoking what the deployment holds outward is [7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md)'s credential inventory, walked one last time.

## Retire a deployment deliberately

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Six steps, ordered so that nothing is released before what it depends on is safe.

Shutting Fleet down is not deleting the server. Devices remain enrolled, credentials remain live, and external services still hold assignments made in your name.

A defensible order:

1. **Decide what must be retained**, and for how long: activity history, escrowed credentials, inventory. Export it while Fleet still runs, because afterwards it is a restore exercise.
2. **Release devices**, or hand them to whatever manages them next. Leaving them enrolled to a server that has stopped answering is the worst outcome for the people using them. What "release" means is platform-specific; the section below covers each one.
3. **Release external assignments**: enrolment profiles, purchased-app tokens, calendar and ticket integrations.
4. **Revoke credentials outward**, including anything held by an identity provider ([7.6](7.6-maintain-credentials-certificates-and-access.md)). If a Fleet MCP server pointed at this deployment, stop it and revoke its API-only token and client bearer too ([6.6](../06-automate-fleet/6.6-connect-fleet-to-an-ai-assistant.md)); neither expires on its own, so an unrevoked one keeps answering against a Fleet that is supposed to be gone.
5. **Take a final backup and store it with its key material**, because a database without the key retires nothing; it just becomes unreadable at a time of its own choosing.
6. **Then decommission**, and delete on your retention schedule rather than immediately.

**Steps two and three are the ones that hurt if skipped**, because they involve devices and services you no longer control.

## Release devices, per platform

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Step two has no single mechanism. How completely one action releases a device, and whether anything is left holding an assignment upstream, differs by platform.

**macOS, iOS and iPadOS.** Un-enrolling from Apple MDM is the destructive `profiles remove` step already covered in [8.4.4](../08-troubleshooting/8.4-host-side-investigation.md#844-macos-mdm-truth-from-the-host); running it, or wiping the device, ends MDM management on that host. That is only half of it. Deleting the Apple Business token from Fleet ([2.10](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.10-apple-mdm-configuration.md#connecting-apple-business)) removes Fleet's own record of the connection; it does not deauthorize anything on Apple's side, because that deletion never calls Apple at all. Devices stay assigned to that server in Apple Business until someone removes the MDM server entry there directly, so a decommission that stops at the Fleet-side token deletion leaves every device still pointed at a server that no longer answers. Do both: un-enroll or wipe the devices, and remove the server assignment in Apple Business itself.

**Windows.** [2.11](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.11-configure-windows-management.md#turning-windows-mdm-off) already documents both halves together: the blast-radius warning before turning Windows MDM off, and the uninstall step that unregisters MDM and then removes the fleetd agent in the same run. Follow that chapter's sequence rather than doing the two separately.

**Android.** This is the one platform where a single Fleet action reaches both ends. Turning Android MDM off, covered in [2.12](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.12-bind-android-enterprise.md#turning-android-mdm-off-deletes-your-enterprise), requests deletion of the Android Enterprise at Google and clears Fleet's own record, marking every Android host unenrolled in the same operation. Fleet does not read back the result of that request, so confirm in the Google Admin console that the enterprise is actually gone before treating the platform as decommissioned.

**ChromeOS.** Fleet does not manage ChromeOS devices at all ([3.7](../03-connect-devices/3.7-enroll-chromeos-devices.md)); the reporting extension is deployed, and removed, entirely through Chrome policy in the Google Admin console. Un-force-installing it, or moving the device out of the organisational unit it was scoped to, stops reporting. There is no Fleet-side action for this platform.

**Linux.** Fleet has no MDM story on Linux, so release means removing the fleetd agent itself: stop and disable its service, then remove its files and package. There is no enrollment profile to revoke.

**Confirm the release from the platform's own side, not only from Fleet.** A host can show unenrolled in Fleet while still holding an assignment upstream, which is exactly the Apple case above: check Apple Business's device list, the Google Admin console, or Windows' own MDM enrollment status directly, rather than trusting Fleet's record alone. For fleetd removal on any platform, confirm the agent process and its scheduled task or service entry are both gone, not just that a command exited without error.
