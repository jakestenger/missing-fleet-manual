# Back up, restore, and retire service state

**Fleet ships no backup tooling, no restore verification and no point-in-time recovery.** Everything in this chapter is either your database vendor's, your storage vendor's, or your own procedure. What Fleet supplies is the map: where the state is, what depends on what, and which parts are useless without something you might not have thought to keep.

Two things make Fleet different from an ordinary web application here, and both are below. **Some of its most valuable state is encrypted with a key that does not live in the database.** And **a restored Fleet does not sit quietly**: it will act, on real devices and real external services, unless you stop it.

## Define the recovery objective and failure scope

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Decide what you are protecting against before deciding how.

Three questions, and they are yours rather than Fleet's:

1. **Which failures are covered?** Accidental deletion, a corrupted database, a lost region, a compromised deployment. They need different answers.
2. **How much time may pass** between the failure and Fleet serving again?
3. **How much data may be lost?**

Fleet publishes no recovery-time or data-loss target, and neither does this manual. **What you can take from here is what each answer costs**, and the constraint that shapes all of them: the parts of Fleet's state that cannot be recovered from a database backup alone.

## Inventory the complete recovery set

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Four places, and only two of them are obvious.

| Where | What is there | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **MySQL** | Nearly everything: configuration, hosts, policies, activity, escrowed credentials | The system of record |
| **Object storage** | Installer bytes, icons, bootstrap packages | Referenced from MySQL, stored separately |
| **Redis** | Live query results, counters, pending notification sets | See below. Mostly not worth backing up |
| **Outside all three** | **The server private key, certificates, and your server configuration** | The part people forget, and the part that makes the rest usable |

### The server private key, and what it does not cover

Fleet encrypts a set of stored assets with a server private key that **lives in your configuration, not in the database**. Restore MySQL without it and those assets are ciphertext.

> **The three disk-encryption escrow populations do not share one chain, and this is the most important paragraph in the chapter.**
>
> - **Linux** recovery material is encrypted under the server private key directly.
> - **macOS** FileVault escrow goes through the Apple certificate authority key, which is itself protected by the server private key. Two hops.
> - **Windows** BitLocker **does not use the server private key at all.** It depends on the Windows enrolment identity certificate and its key, which live in your server configuration.
>
> **So a perfect MySQL restore with the correct server private key still loses BitLocker recovery** unless you separately preserved that enrolment material. [5.8](../05-manage-devices/5.8-enforce-disk-encryption-and-manage-recovery-credentials.md) explains what those credentials are worth; this is where you make sure you can still read them.

**Back up the configuration as material, not as documentation.** The private key, the certificates and their keys, the secret-store references, and the infrastructure definitions that create the rest.

### The failure modes are inverted

Worth knowing before an incident rather than during one:

- **A wrong key** fails at the point something encrypted is touched. That may be startup, or it may be later, when a feature that needs it is used.
- **A missing key** is worse, because Fleet may start cleanly. Depending on what else is configured it can come up, serve, and **persist a disabled Apple MDM status into the database.** A key-less binary pointed at a production database can therefore write a disabling change into production.
- **The health endpoint does not test the key or whether anything encrypted is readable.** It may stay green throughout.

**Never point a binary at a production database without the production key.** That is the whole instruction, and it is easy to violate while testing a restore.

## Protect MySQL and object storage coherently

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting.svg) Two stores, two mechanisms, two moments.

Use your database vendor's procedures. Fleet has no opinion and provides no tooling.

**The gap that matters is between the two stores.** They are snapshotted separately, so any restore has a window where one is newer than the other, in one of two directions:

- **A database row whose bytes are missing.** An installer Fleet believes it has and cannot deliver.
- **An object with no row.** Storage Fleet does not know about.

**Fleet detects and repairs neither.** Document the consistency gap your procedure produces, and order the two captures deliberately rather than by accident.

> **One direction is worse than the other.** A cleanup job removes stale unreferenced installer, icon and bootstrap objects. Restore a **database older than your storage** and objects the older database does not reference become candidates for deletion. Whether that is recoverable depends entirely on whether your object store keeps versions. **Check that before you need it.**

## Plan Redis loss and restart

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Decide deliberately, because both answers are defensible.

Redis holds live query results as messages rather than records, plus counters and pending notification sets. Almost all of it is reconstructible or expendable.

**What losing it costs**: live query results in flight are gone, pending notification sets are gone so the deliveries they represented never happen ([6.5](../06-automate-fleet/6.5-integrations-webhooks-and-external-workflows.md)), and rate-limit and error counters reset.

**Neither answer is wrong.** Persisting Redis makes recovery quieter; starting empty is faster and loses only what is listed above. **Choose from those consequences rather than from a general preference for durability**, and write down which you chose so a person recovering at three in the morning does not have to work it out.

## Restore into an isolated environment

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting.svg) This is the section that will save somebody's week.

**A restored Fleet is not passive.** There is no read-only mode, no global switch that stops scheduled work, and nothing that tells Fleet it is a copy.

Individual schedules have their own gates and timings, so not everything starts at once. But **enough starts**, and it acts on the real world:

> A restored instance can, without any device involvement:
>
> - **Drain its restored job queue** into real tickets in your real ticketing system.
> - **Reconcile Apple enrollment assignments** against Apple, changing what production devices are assigned to.
> - **Create and delete calendar events** on real people's real calendars.
> - **Send webhooks** to real destinations.
>
> None of that requires a device to check in. It requires Fleet to start.

**The only control that covers all of it is network isolation.** Configuration will not do it, because the settings live in the database you just restored.

**So: deny egress before you start the restored instance.** Not after, not once you have checked something. Before. Block outbound access to push services, enterprise mobility services, identity providers, ticketing, calendar and every webhook destination, and keep it blocked for the life of the copy.

## Verify semantic recovery

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Proving a restore is good, rather than proving it is present.

In order:

1. **The version and migration state** are what you expect ([7.2](7.2-upgrade-fleet-and-fleetd.md)).
2. **Sign in**, which proves the database and the session machinery.
3. **Representative records exist**: a fleet, some hosts, a policy, a configuration profile.
4. **An object can be retrieved**, which tests the storage half and the reference between them.
5. **Something encrypted can be decrypted.**

That last one is the point of the exercise, and it needs care.

> **Do not prove decryptability by counting what Fleet says is decryptable.** The job that verifies stored disk-encryption credentials does not re-test rows already marked one way or the other, so a restored database keeps whatever the flags said before. **Those counts are inherited, not measured.**
>
> Prove it by **retrieving one credential per platform** in the isolated environment and confirming you got a value. One from each of the three, because they use three different key chains and one working does not imply another.
>
> **Confirm you received a value; you do not need to display it or use it.** That is enough to establish the chain is intact, and it keeps the exercise inside the disclosure line the rest of this manual holds to.

## Set retention and deletion order

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) What Fleet expires, and the larger set it does not.

Fleet has retention settings, and their coverage is narrower than their names suggest.

> **Activity expiry does not remove host-linked activities.** Every policy-automation activity is host-linked by construction, so a thirty-day window leaves them in place indefinitely ([5.9](../05-manage-devices/5.9-automate-remediation-with-policies.md)). They become eligible only once the host is deleted and the link goes with it.

**Deleting a host is three different operations at once**: some rows are removed, some are marked deleted and stay, and some are left orphaned for a later sweep. If a deletion has to be complete for a legal or contractual reason, verify it rather than assuming it.

**Coordinate the layers.** Fleet's expiry, your database's growth, your object store's lifecycle rules, and whatever your external log destination does are four independent policies. The strictest one does not govern; each one governs its own copy.

## Rehearse disaster recovery

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) The only way any of this becomes true.

**Measure, do not assume.** Time an actual restore and record what was lost. That measurement is your real recovery target, whatever the one in your plan says.

A rehearsal worth running covers: the database and the object store together, **from a real backup rather than a copy of production**; the private key and certificates supplied separately, to prove you have them; **egress denied throughout**; the five verification steps above, including one credential retrieval per platform; and an honest note of everything you had to look up, because that is the runbook you did not have.

**Topology failure is [7.5](7.5-maintain-capacity-and-availability.md)'s**: writer promotion, traffic, regional recovery. This chapter's rehearsal answers a narrower question, which is whether the state you kept is the state you needed.
