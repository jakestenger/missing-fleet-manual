# Control operating system updates

**Fleet does not install operating system updates.** It publishes a requirement, or an update policy the platform already understands, and the operating system does everything else: deciding what is available, downloading it, prompting the user, honouring deferrals, and restarting the machine. Fleet finds out afterwards, by looking at inventory.

There is one exception, and it shapes the chapter. On **macOS 13 and earlier** Fleet's agent supplies, configures and launches Nudge, a prompting tool. Nudge is not the installer either, but it is a Fleet-owned stage with its own way of failing, so those Macs behave differently from every other device you manage.

**Fleet's OS-update enforcement controls are Premium**, and the built-in settings page needs Apple or Windows MDM already working, with access limited to global and team administrators. The Linux alternatives at the end of this chapter, inventory and policies, are not ([2.10](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.10-apple-mdm-configuration.md), [2.11](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.11-configure-windows-management.md)).

## Understand the requirement-to-observation control loop

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Six stages, and the reason to keep them separate is that three different things all get called "updated".

1. You publish a setting or a custom profile.
2. Fleet validates known fields, your licence, the MDM prerequisites, and the built-in against custom exclusions it implements. It does not referee every possible conflict; several are resolved only at reconciliation, or not at all.
3. Fleet materialises the platform payload, or merges it into one.
4. The payload reaches an eligible device.
5. **The operating system evaluates availability and runs its own prompting, installation and restart.**
6. Fleet later receives inventory showing what version the device is on.

Stage 5 is the whole middle of the process, and **the Fleet server has no visibility into platform installation or restart**. The one narrow exception is on macOS 13 and earlier, where fleetd logs its own Nudge launch attempts locally.

### The evidence ladder

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Three distinct claims, and it is worth being deliberate about which one you have:

| Claim | What supports it |
|---|---|
| **A requirement is configured** | The activity record of you changing the setting |
| **A policy reached the device** | Profile delivery status, **for custom profiles only** |
| **A device is actually on the version** | OS inventory, collected periodically |

**Built-in enforcement skips the middle rung entirely.** The profiles Fleet generates for built-in OS updates are hidden: they do not appear in profile listings, in host details, or in the aggregate summary, on either Apple or Windows. That is deliberate, because they are Fleet's to manage rather than yours to edit, but the consequence is real. **You get no delivery evidence for built-in enforcement.** You get the activity record, and later you get inventory.

**A successful save proves that Fleet accepted the input, not that the target platform can apply it.** Several of the traps below are instances of that.

## Choose built-in enforcement or a custom update profile

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) These are not one control with different field names per platform. They express genuinely different things.

| | What built-in lets you say | Custom mechanism | Targeting |
|---|---|---|---|
| **macOS, iOS, iPadOS** | A minimum version **and** an absolute deadline date | Apple software-update declaration | Built-in: fleet only |
| **Windows** | A deadline in days **and** a restart grace period. **No version** | Windows Update CSP profile | Built-in: fleet only |
| **Android** | Nothing. There is no built-in form | `systemUpdate` in an Android profile | Profile labels |
| **Linux** | Nothing | Nothing | |

Two consequences follow immediately. **You cannot pin a Windows version through Fleet's built-in control**: it expresses urgency, not a target. And **Android has no built-in path at all**, so everything in that section is a configuration profile.

### Targeting is the other half of the choice

**Built-in settings are scoped by fleet, and their generated payloads do not accept labels.** Custom profiles get label scope like any other profile ([5.2](5.2-manage-configuration-profiles-and-declarative-settings.md)).

If your rollout plan needs rings inside a fleet, that decides the mechanism for you before any other consideration ([5.1](5.1-plan-target-and-govern-device-changes.md)).

### They exclude each other, and the boundary is wider than you would guess

**On Apple, the exclusion is family-wide.** Any configured built-in macOS, iOS **or** iPadOS setting blocks a custom Apple software-update declaration, and a tracked custom declaration blocks the built-in settings. Not per platform. Configure a minimum iOS version and you have blocked custom macOS update declarations too.

**On Windows, the exclusion triggers on the entire `Update` CSP subtree.** A custom profile that sets nothing but active hours still collides with built-in settings, because Fleet is guarding the whole area rather than the specific nodes it writes.

Fleet permits multiple custom Apple declarations, and multiple custom Windows profiles. Keeping those from contradicting each other is your job, not Fleet's.

## Enforce Apple operating-system versions and deadlines

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) Two mechanisms behind one settings form, split at **macOS 14**.

### macOS 14 and later, iOS 17 and later, iPadOS 17 and later

Fleet generates a declaration carrying an exact target version and a deadline at **noon, local to the device**.

**Minimum and target are different things and the distinction matters.** What you type is a minimum: it decides which hosts need action. What Fleet sends is that exact version as the target. A device below the minimum is told to go to precisely that version, not to the newest one available.

Fleet checks your version against Apple's published list and refuses one that does not exist, saying the minimum version is not supported by Apple. **That check does not run on the fleet-spec path**, which is narrower than it sounds: the global and Unassigned GitOps paths do validate, and the per-fleet spec endpoint does not. On that endpoint iOS and iPadOS get no validation at all, not even the format check. So the same configuration is rejected through the interface and accepted through that path, and a version with a typo can be stored and then never satisfied.

**Rapid Security Responses are visible but not targetable**, and the two halves of Fleet disagree about them. Server-side comparisons strip the parenthesised suffix, so `13.3.1 (a)` and `13.3.1` are the same version for enforcement, and you cannot require an RSR. **The host vitals comparison in the browser does not strip it**, and reads the patch component as zero instead. So a Mac carrying an RSR, which is more patched than your minimum rather than less, can display as failing to meet it. Trust the enforcement behaviour over that indicator, and do not chase a device it flags without checking the version yourself.

### What the user experiences

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) Apple owns all of this, and it is worth knowing before you choose a deadline.

As the deadline approaches the device notifies the user and prepares the download. **At the deadline on macOS, the operating system force quits every open application, whether or not documents are unsaved.** That single behaviour should decide which fleet you roll out to first, and what you tell people beforehand.

On iOS and iPadOS, a passcode-protected device requires the passcode to be entered at the enforcement date before the update can proceed, unless it was entered earlier.

On Apple silicon, enforcement may be authorised by a bootstrap token. Without one, macOS prompts the user for credentials instead, which is a different experience and a different failure mode ([2.10](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.10-apple-mdm-configuration.md)).

A device that was offline or interrupted at the deadline does not stay stuck: Apple retries after it reconnects, with an installation attempt within about an hour.

### macOS 13 and earlier: the Nudge path

Fleet has no declarative mechanism on these versions. Instead, fleetd downloads, configures and launches **Nudge**, which prompts the user with increasing insistence as the deadline nears. It requires fleetd with agent updates enabled; a host without them never gets the mechanism at all.

**The deadline is a different clock.** Nudge's deadline is **20:00 UTC**, which Fleet's source describes as noon Pacific Standard Time. It is not adjusted for daylight saving, so it lands at 13:00 Pacific for half the year, and at whatever that translates to everywhere else. Two Macs in your fleet on either side of macOS 14 will therefore hit their deadlines at different times of day.

> **A Nudge launch failure is permanent for that agent process, and Fleet will not tell you.** If launching Nudge exits with an error, fleetd stops trying for the rest of that process's life. Nothing is sent to the server, no activity is written, and no host field changes. The setting still reads as configured.
>
> You can find it, but only by going to look: fleetd logs the line, and it is queryable through the `fleetd_logs` table ([4.2](../04-know-your-devices/4.2-run-queries-and-reports.md)). If you enforce updates on older Macs, make that query part of your routine rather than something you run after someone complains.

### Updating newly enrolled Apple devices

A separate switch asks Apple to update a device during automated enrollment. Three things about it are easy to get wrong:

- **It requests Apple's latest available release, not your configured target.** A device set up this way can land on a newer version than your minimum.
- Where no minimum is configured, it applies to every eligible device rather than only those below a threshold.
- **It defaults to on** when it is omitted from configuration while both a macOS version and a deadline are set. That is a backward-compatibility rule, and it means automation can enable behaviour nobody wrote down.

There is no equivalent switch for iOS or iPadOS.

## Configure Windows update deadlines and grace periods

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) You supply two numbers: a **deadline** of 0 to 30 days and a **restart grace period** of 0 to 7 days. There is no version field. Both must be set together or both cleared.

**Zero is a configured value, not "off".** A deadline of zero means enforce immediately, not do not enforce. To stop enforcing, clear both fields.

### Six settings, not two

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference.svg) Fleet writes six policies in a single atomic block, and **three of them you never see**:

| Setting | Value | Visible to you |
|---|---|---|
| Deadline for feature updates | your deadline | Yes |
| Deadline for quality updates | your deadline, the same number | Yes |
| Deadline grace period | your grace period | Yes |
| Automatic updates allowed | on | **No** |
| **Pause access disabled** | on | **No** |
| `ConfigureDeadlineNoAutoReboot` | `1` | **No** |

**The fifth row deserves a decision rather than a discovery.** Turning on Fleet's built-in Windows enforcement **removes the user's ability to pause updates**, through a setting the form never mentions. That may be exactly what you want. It should not be a surprise.

Note also that **the same deadline governs both feature and quality updates.** Fleet does not let you separate them; a custom profile does.

### How the deadline and grace period actually interact

They are not sequential. **The deadline counts from when the device discovers the update. The grace period counts from when a restart becomes pending.** The forced restart happens at whichever of those two moments is *later*.

So grace does not add days onto the end of your deadline in the normal case. It only extends anything when installation finishes so late that the restart-pending moment plus the grace period lands beyond the original deadline. Set a seven-day deadline and a two-day grace expecting nine days, and you will usually get seven.

**Once the effective deadline is reached, Windows can force the restart regardless of active hours.** Active hours shape when restarts happen before that point, not after it.

Microsoft's own documentation is inconsistent on the deadline model in one place, where the prose for an older Windows build disagrees with its own parenthetical. The model above is the one Microsoft states in its compliance-deadline guidance.

### Custom Windows profiles

A custom Update CSP profile is the way to express what the built-in form cannot: separate feature and quality deferrals, version pinning, update schedules, active hours, and notification behaviour ([5.2](5.2-manage-configuration-profiles-and-declarative-settings.md) owns the authoring).

> **Watch what `Verified` means here.** For a custom Windows profile, Fleet marks it verified when the device reports the write succeeded. **It does not read the value back.** So Verified means the device applied your instruction at the time, not that the setting is still in force, and certainly not that any update completed.

### One risk worth testing rather than assuming

Fleet sends all six settings as one atomic block. If a device rejects any one of them, Windows fails the whole block and attempts to roll the others back, and that rollback can itself fail and leave the device partially configured.

**Fleet sends the split nodes**, `ConfigureDeadlineForFeatureUpdates` and `ConfigureDeadlineForQualityUpdates`, which are the two Microsoft's current CSP reference documents. It does not send the combined `ConfigureDeadlineNoAutoReboot` node, so the compatibility question that node raises does not arise here. **Microsoft does not document what an unsupported policy node returns**, which is why the atomic-block risk above is still worth testing rather than assuming.

**So test the generated profile on every Windows build and edition you actually run**, and inspect the device's policy state afterwards rather than trusting the status in Fleet. The registry path under `PolicyManager` is the most followable read-back; the Windows MDM diagnostic report is another.

## Configure Android system-update policy

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg) **Fleet 4.90.1 has no built-in Android form.** Android OS-update control is a `systemUpdate` block inside an Android configuration profile, and it is Premium-gated independently of everything else.

Three modes are available: taking updates automatically, taking them inside a maintenance window, or postponing them. **Postponing means 30 days**, not a period you choose. Freeze periods have hard vendor limits: **at most 90 days each, at least 60 days between them, no overlapping**, and the calculation ignores leap years. They **repeat every year**, so a freeze set for a December release window returns each December until you remove it.

One side effect worth knowing: choosing the windowed mode **also constrains Google Play application updates**, not only system updates. Nothing in the field name suggests that.

> **A misspelled field inside `systemUpdate` is accepted and silently dropped.** Fleet does not strictly validate the nested keys, so a maintenance window with a mistyped field name applies, reports success, and simply is not there. Check the device's behaviour rather than the profile's status.

**Fleet does not check device ownership for this policy.** Google's model is that only a device owner can control system updates; a profile owner, which is what manages a personally owned work profile, can report on them but not control them. Fleet accepts the profile either way. Apply system-update policy to fully managed devices, and do not assume Fleet will stop you doing otherwise.

## State the Linux update-management boundary

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation.svg) **Fleet does not enforce Linux operating system updates.** There is no built-in setting, no update profile, no deadline, and no restart policy.

There is a second consequence worth stating, because it affects how you check anything: **Linux versions do not appear in the Current versions table** on the OS updates page. A Linux reader following the verification advice later in this chapter would find a table that excludes them.

What you do have is inventory and testing. Fleet knows what version each Linux host is running ([4.1](../04-know-your-devices/4.1-understand-hosts-vitals-and-inventory.md)), and a policy can test it ([4.3](../04-know-your-devices/4.3-use-policies-for-compliance.md)). Installation remains the distribution's own tooling. A script ([5.3](5.3-run-and-manage-scripts.md)) or policy automation ([5.9](5.9-automate-remediation-with-policies.md)) can drive that tooling, but that is you building something rather than Fleet providing an equivalent.

## Handle conflicts, offline hosts, retries, interruption, and limits

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting.svg)

**Exclusions.** Apple's is family-wide; Windows' covers the whole `Update` subtree. Fleet permits multiple custom profiles of either kind and will not referee between them.

**Android field collisions.** Fleet merges Android policy fragments by **profile name in alphabetical order**, and a later profile wins a duplicate top-level field. The profile that loses is **marked failed in its entirety**, including for fields it won. Two profiles that both set `systemUpdate` do not merge; one wins and the other reports as a failure.

**Offline at the deadline.** Apple retries after the device reconnects. For Windows, Microsoft does not clearly document what happens to a device returning from offline past its deadline. One thing is worth knowing while you test it: **time offline is not automatically time overdue**, because a device that never discovered the update never started its deadline clock.

**Interrupted installation.** Downloads, installations and restarts belong to the platform. Fleet cannot cancel one, cannot roll one back, and has no visibility into one in progress.

**Removing enforcement.** Clear the required field pair to remove built-in settings; remove or rescope a custom profile as any other ([5.2](5.2-manage-configuration-profiles-and-declarative-settings.md)). What stops is future enforcement. **What cannot be undone is anything already installed and any restart already taken.**

**The two macOS paths degrade differently, and neither stops instantly.** On macOS 14 and later, MDM connectivity is needed to *deliver* or *revise* a declaration, but Apple's declarative model is device-autonomous once the declaration is present: a device already holding one keeps enforcing it. Withdrawal likewise only takes effect once the removal reaches the device, so an offline Mac stays under the old requirement. On macOS 13 and earlier it is fleetd and agent updates that matter, not MDM. A single failure does not affect both halves of a mixed fleet the same way.

## Roll out, verify, revise, and withdraw update enforcement

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto.svg)

### Roll out within the scope your mechanism allows

Built-in enforcement moves by fleet. Custom profiles move by fleet and label. Pilot on hardware and OS branches you actually run, and think about restart-sensitive machines before rather than after.

### Verify delivery and convergence separately

**What is genuinely available to you:**

| Check | What it tells you | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Activity record** | The setting changed, and to what | Global activity feed |
| **Host vitals** | Whether *this* host meets the minimum, with its deadline | Host details, **Apple only** |
| **Current versions** | Aggregate version spread | OS updates page, **no Linux** |
| **A policy** | Whether hosts pass a version test you wrote | Policies |
| **Device policy state** | What a Windows device actually has | Registry or MDM diagnostic report, on the device |

Two cautions on that table. The **activity types are global-feed only**: they never appear on a host's own timeline, so looking at one host will not show you that its fleet's requirement changed. And **re-saving an unchanged value writes no activity at all**, so an empty feed is not evidence that nothing happened.

**The Current versions timestamp is aggregation time, not report time.** For a healthy, reporting host expect it to lag by roughly a detail interval plus an aggregation interval, on the order of two hours. For an offline host the underlying inventory can be arbitrarily old, and no timestamp on that page tells you which case you are looking at. Drilling into the host list bypasses the aggregation but still reads stored inventory; it is not an on-demand query of the device.

**Define success as the population converging**, not as the settings form saving.

### What is not evidence

- The settings page saving successfully.
- The absence of errors.
- A profile listed as delivered, which built-in enforcement never produces anyway.
- **Windows `Verified`**, which means the write succeeded, not that the value is still set.
- An empty activity feed, which may mean nothing changed.

### Revise or withdraw

Changing a version or deadline is simply a new desired state; Fleet does not track a history of what you used to require. Clear the field pairs to withdraw built-in enforcement, and expect nothing to be rolled back on any device that already acted.
