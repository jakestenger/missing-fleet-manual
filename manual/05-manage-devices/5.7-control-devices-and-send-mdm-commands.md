# Control devices and send custom MDM commands

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation-light.svg) When a device is lost, an employee needs help regaining access, or hardware is being retired, Fleet gives operators a set of direct actions: lock, unlock, locate, clear a passcode, and wipe where the platform supports them. These controls bring incident response and routine device administration into the same place you use to understand the host.

Before sending an action, check its platform-specific behavior and recovery path. A Mac and a Windows laptop may share a Lock button, but the mechanisms, prerequisites, and results differ. Some actions are irreversible once they reach the device.

Fleet chooses the delivery channel for each supported action. Windows lock and unlock and all Linux actions use scripts; Android uses its management API; Apple and Windows use MDM for the actions described below. You can also send custom Apple or Windows MDM commands when a supported action does not cover the task ([1.2](../01-foundations/1.2-how-fleet-reaches-a-device.md)).

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg) A brief check of the host, the person using it, and the recovery plan helps you act confidently. Use the following checks when preparing a device action.

**Confirm you have the right host.** Serial numbers and hostnames repeat, people hand machines on, and a stale inventory record can point at a device that now belongs to someone else. Check the identity against something the person can confirm.

**Confirm who is using the device.** A lock interrupts that person's access. Establish whether the interruption is intended and how they will regain access if the device is recovered.

**Prepare the recovery path.** Identify who can unlock, recover, or redeploy the device, and make sure they have the required access before the action begins.

**Handle the unlock PIN as a credential.** It releases a lock applied by Fleet; disk-recovery keys serve the separate purpose of unlocking an encrypted volume ([5.8](5.8-enforce-disk-encryption-and-manage-recovery-credentials.md)). Share the PIN through your organization's approved secret-sharing channel and keep it out of ordinary tickets and chat.

**Be careful with bulk.** These actions are per host by design. Anything that applies one of them to many hosts at once deserves a second person looking at the target list.

**Wipe is not the only irreversible thing you can do from Fleet.** A script runs with full administrative privilege ([5.3](5.3-run-and-manage-scripts.md)), and a raw custom command can do anything the platform allows.

## Choose a supported device action or a custom MDM command

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation-light.svg) Start with a supported action when Fleet provides one. Use a custom command when you need to supply a platform payload yourself.

For a **supported action**, Fleet selects the mechanism, checks the preconditions it knows about, and tracks the states it models. The available state information still varies: Android lock has no locked state in Fleet, and clearing an Apple passcode has no device-level pending state.

A **custom command** lets you send your own payload and retrieve the platform's response. Fleet relays it without interpreting its intended effect.

> **Use the supported action for anything destructive.** Sending the equivalent raw command can erase a device **without Fleet's device status ever transitioning to wiped**. The status stays where it was until some other tracked event moves it. That applies to a raw Apple erase and to a raw Windows remote wipe alike. If you have a reason to send one anyway, verify the outcome independently rather than trusting what Fleet shows.

The custom path also loses the precondition checks, the refusal on personally enrolled devices, the unlock PIN, the conflict detection, and the activity that records a wipe. It keeps the licence gate. **Do not rely on a raw command to tidy up a host's queued work**: a raw Apple erase can trigger that cleanup once its result arrives, and a raw Windows remote wipe does not.

## Compare platform support, prerequisites, transport, and licence

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference-light.svg) What is possible, per platform.

| | macOS | iOS and iPadOS | Windows | Linux | Android |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Lock** | Apple MDM | Apple MDM, as **Lost Mode** | **Script** | **Script** | Android API |
| **Unlock** | **No command.** Fleet gives you a PIN | Apple MDM | **Script** | **Script** | **Not from Fleet.** Local only |
| **Wipe** | Apple MDM | Apple MDM | **OMA-DM** | **Script** | Android API |
| **Locate** | No | Yes, with Lost Mode | No | No | No |
| **Clear passcode** | **Refused** | Yes | No | No | Yes |

ChromeOS supports none of it.

These platform differences affect how you plan recovery:

- **iOS and iPadOS "lock" is Lost Mode**, not the same mechanism as a Mac lock. It is what makes locating possible, and it behaves like Apple's feature rather than like Fleet's.
- **macOS unlock is not a command at all.** Fleet hands you a PIN and somebody types it on the machine. If the Mac is not in front of anyone, it stays locked.
- **Android has no remote unlock.** You can lock an Android device from Fleet and you cannot reverse it from Fleet; the person holding it unlocks it locally.

Windows lock and unlock require Windows MDM to be enabled even though they execute as scripts; Linux lock and unlock do not. All script-backed actions need fleetd with scripts enabled ([5.3](5.3-run-and-manage-scripts.md)). Check the licence column as well: most actions here require Premium, while Android wipe on a company-owned device is available on Free.

<a id="eligibility-before-you-find-out-the-hard-way"></a>

### Check action eligibility

| Action | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **iOS and iPadOS Lost Mode** | **Company-owned, automatic enrollment.** Manual and personal enrollments are refused |
| **Apple clear passcode** | iOS or iPadOS, not personally enrolled, and Fleet must already hold an unlock token for the device |
| **Android wipe** | **Company-owned only.** A personally owned Android is unenrolled instead, which removes management rather than erasing the device |
| **Android lock and clear passcode** | Both ownership models |
| **Windows unlock** | Somebody must power the machine back on, because lock shut it down and the unlock script is queued work waiting for an agent that is not running |
| **Linux lock** | Root is exempt, and only ordinary user accounts are password-locked. Test the result against your own login stack before relying on it |
| **Any of them** | Administrator or maintainer for the relevant scope. **GitOps is refused**: each action checks the ability to list hosts before the MDM-command write, and GitOps lacks it ([a.4](../09-appendices/a.4-roles-and-permissions-matrix.md)). Refusal of these actions is not containment: a GitOps credential can still send raw MDM commands, including device-destroying ones on Premium ([a.4](../09-appendices/a.4-roles-and-permissions-matrix.md), [1.4](../01-foundations/1.4-identity-and-roles.md)) |

Windows lock powers the machine off. Someone must restart it before the agent can receive the queued unlock instruction, so arrange physical access as part of the recovery plan.

## Follow each action from request to result

![Explanation](../_assets/icons/explanation-light.svg) Follow the delivery channel when investigating a pending action. It tells you which component must receive the work and where to look next ([8.1](../08-troubleshooting/8.1-diagnostic-method.md)).

**Apple, by MDM.** Fleet enqueues the command, pushes a notification, and the device collects it on its next check-in. A device that is off or offline gets it whenever it reappears.

**Windows lock and unlock, by script.** The request enters the host's queue like any other script and runs when the agent next collects work ([5.1](5.1-plan-target-and-govern-device-changes.md)).

**Windows wipe, by OMA-DM.** A different channel from the Windows lock beside it, and it depends on the agent waking the device's management client. That dependency is the thing to check first when a Windows wipe sits pending.

**Linux, by script.** Everything on Linux is the agent running a shell script. No MDM is involved at any point.

**Android, by its management API.** Fleet issues an AMAPI command, not a policy update, and updates its record when the command result arrives over Pub/Sub.

## Lock, unlock, locate, and clear passcodes

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg)

### macOS

Fleet generates a **six-digit PIN** and locks the machine. Somebody physically present enters that PIN to release it. Keep the caution from the opening section in mind: that PIN is credential material and deserves the handling you would give any other secret.

Clearing a passcode is **refused on macOS**. It is an iOS and iPadOS operation.

### iOS and iPadOS

Lock puts the device into **Lost Mode**, which is why location works here and nowhere else. Fleet can ask for the device's position and show you where it last reported.

**A device in Lost Mode that cannot reach Fleet cannot be released by Fleet.** The unlock is a command like any other, so it waits for the device to check in. A phone that is off, out of coverage, or otherwise unable to reach you stays locked until that changes.

Clearing a passcode works here, subject to ownership and token requirements.

### Windows

Windows implements lock and unlock with scripts that change local access settings. Review their effects before using them in your environment:

> **Windows lock shuts the machine down.** It is not a screen lock; the device powers off.
>
> **Windows unlock re-enables every disabled local account on the machine**, not only the one the lock disabled. An account somebody disabled deliberately, for an entirely unrelated reason, comes back. It also resets the cached-logons setting to a fixed value regardless of what your organization had configured.

After an unlock, review any local accounts your organization intentionally kept disabled and restore the intended cached-logon configuration. Include those steps in the Windows recovery procedure.

### Linux

Linux also uses scripts for lock and unlock. Unlock does not restore the autologin configuration disabled by lock, so restore it separately if your intended configuration requires it.

### Android

Fleet can lock an Android device. The person holding it then unlocks it locally with its own PIN; Fleet provides no remote unlock action.

Fleet does not track Android's current lock state. Its interface can therefore show the device as unlocked immediately after a successful lock command. Use the command result to establish that the request completed, and confirm the current screen state on the device when needed.

One consequence follows. On every other platform a locked device must be unlocked before it can be wiped. On Android, wipe on a locked device is permitted, because Fleet does not model the lock.

## Wipe a device and plan for what remains

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg)

> **Wipe is irreversible on every platform, but it does not erase the same thing on every platform.** Read the platform notes below before sending it; once the command reaches the device, Fleet cannot cancel or roll it back.

**Apple** erases the device through its own mechanism, and it behaves the way Apple's erase behaves.

**Windows** uses a remote wipe through OMA-DM, which is a different channel from the lock beside it in the interface.

Linux wipe uses a script with two limits that matter for device retirement:

- **It is path-based deletion, not a secure erase.** The script deletes selected paths and snapshots **without overwriting the underlying storage**, and untargeted partitions and mountpoints can survive. **Do not treat a Linux wipe as sanitisation** for a machine leaving your control.
- **Fleet reports success before deletion starts.** The reporting process hands work to a detached child, which waits ten seconds before deleting data. That early success confirms neither the start nor completion of deletion; verify the outcome independently.

**Android** wipes within the scope its ownership model allows, and Android wipe on a company-owned device is one of the few things here that does not need Premium.

After a successful tracked wipe, the host record remains in Fleet and its remaining upcoming activities are cancelled. If the device is recovered and re-enrolled, Fleet clears the computed wiped state and begins the enrollment lifecycle again ([3.1](../03-connect-devices/3.1-enrollment-design-and-host-lifecycle.md)). Deleting the retained host record is a separate administrative choice.

## Turn MDM off for one host

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg) You can remove MDM from an individual supported host without changing the platform-wide integration. This action is available on Fleet Free and Premium wherever the platform supports it.

Apple hosts (macOS, iOS, iPadOS) and BYOD-enrolled Android hosts can have MDM turned off individually, from the host's Actions menu. **Windows refuses outright**, with its own error ("Can't turn off MDM for Windows hosts"), and every other platform, ChromeOS included, gets a generic unsupported-platform response. **Company-owned Android does not offer the action at all**; Wipe is what an administrator sends to a device the organization owns instead.

The button's label tracks what happens next, not the platform. On macOS it reads **Turn off**: the host stays enrolled with Fleet and keeps checking in, only MDM management leaves. On iOS, iPadOS, and Android it reads **Unenroll**, because there is no such split there: removing management takes the device out of Fleet's device-management view too, deleting the settings and apps Fleet placed, or on Android, deleting the work profile and its company data.

**Requires administrator or maintainer**, globally or on the host's own fleet; GitOps cannot invoke it, the same restriction [above](#check-action-eligibility) gives lock, unlock, and wipe.

> The timing of unenrollment differs by platform. For Apple devices, Fleet queues the removal command and immediately marks the host MDM-unenrolled in its database. The device applies the removal when it next checks in, as the confirmation dialog explains. For Android BYOD, Fleet sends a work-profile wipe command and records unenrollment only after the device confirms completion.

**What survives, on macOS and iOS/iPadOS:** disk-encryption keys and any bootstrap-package record, kept intentionally in case the host re-enrolls ([5.8](5.8-enforce-disk-encryption-and-manage-recovery-credentials.md) covers what a re-enrolled host does and does not get back). **What does not:** every profile Fleet placed is deleted from Fleet's side (the device stops acknowledging removal requests once it is gone, so Fleet does not wait for it), MDM-delivered certificates are dropped, queued MDM-dependent activities including pending app installs are cancelled, and the stored Recovery Lock password is discarded, because Apple itself wipes the device-side lock when the enrollment profile is removed.

**Getting the device back depends on how it enrolled, not on this action.** A **Mac** that enrolled through Apple Business Manager's automatic device enrollment recovers on its own: it stays enrolled with Fleet and keeps checking in, so once its agent sees that it is still assigned in Apple Business Manager but no longer MDM-enrolled, Fleet prompts it to renew its enrollment profile, which the end user or an administrator can force with `sudo profiles renew -type enrollment`. Reserve a reset for a Mac that is no longer running Fleet's agent. An **iPhone or iPad** that enrolled the same way re-enrolls only after it is erased and taken back through Setup Assistant, since it runs no agent to renew enrollment on its own. Either way, confirm the device is still assigned in Apple Business Manager first, since leaving MDM off does nothing by itself. A manually enrolled iOS or iPadOS device re-enrolls by going through **Hosts > Add hosts > iOS/iPadOS** again. A BYOD account-driven enrollment re-enrolls from the device itself, at **Settings > General > VPN & Device Management > Sign in to Work or School Account**. A manually enrolled Mac's end user follows the **Turn on MDM** instructions on their own **My device** page. Android re-enrolls the same way it enrolled, through **Hosts > Add hosts > Android**.

## Send custom Apple and Windows MDM commands

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg) When the supported actions do not cover what you need, Fleet will relay a raw command to Apple or Windows devices.

Send each custom-command request to devices on one platform. The server enforces platform matching more strictly than the command-line tool. There is no separate host-count limit, but the request-body size limits how many targets fit in one request.

**Send it** with `fleetctl mdm run-command`, or `POST /api/v1/fleet/commands/run` against the API. Apple expects a plist XML command; Windows expects a single `<Exec>` containing one `<Item>`.

**Command identifiers differ by entry point.** The direct API requires and preserves the identifier in your payload; `fleetctl` replaces it with one of its own. Either way, **keep the identifier you get back**, because it is how you retrieve the result later with `fleetctl get mdm-command-results` or `GET /api/v1/fleet/commands/results`.

An Apple request can report partial success when push notifications fail for some devices. The commands are still queued for those devices, which can collect them at their next check-in. Inspect the reported notification failures before deciding whether a new command is needed.

**Destructive command types are licence-gated**, and the matching differs: Apple matches exact request types, while Windows identifies a remote wipe from the target path in the payload.

> **A raw destructive command does not inherit the supported action's bookkeeping.** Fleet will relay an erase command perfectly well and the device status will not become wiped. If you send one, verify the outcome on the device rather than in Fleet.

## Read status, results, and retry signals

![Reference](../_assets/icons/reference-light.svg) Fleet exposes several kinds of status. Choose the one that answers the question you are investigating:

| Surface | What it actually tells you |
|---|---|
| **The activity feed** | That somebody **requested** the action, at the moment they requested it |
| **Pending action** | That Fleet is expecting something to happen |
| **Device status** | Fleet's computed belief about the actions it tracks. `unlocked` is the **default**, not proof |
| **The command result** | What the device itself said |

An activity records acceptance of the request and is not amended with its later outcome. It can also be absent for a device whose push notification failed even though the command remains queued. Use the command result or tracked device state to assess completion.

The command result contains the device's response. Apple and Windows results are available through the results API; only Apple has the corresponding per-host command feed in the interface.

**Pending** means it is waiting. **Acknowledged** means the device says it did it. **Error** means the device says it did not.

**NotNow** means the device received the command but cannot act yet, for example while it is locked or busy. The command remains available for a later check-in, and Fleet counts those deferrals. Treat it as pending work while investigating what is preventing the device from proceeding.

### Elsewhere

**Windows** redelivers until the device acknowledges. **Android** reports its command result back over Pub/Sub.

**Script-backed actions do not give you an ordinary script result.** Fleet stores the script result internally and consumes its exit code to update the action's state; the lock, unlock and wipe activities do not expose the output and exit code you would see from a script you ran yourself ([5.3](5.3-run-and-manage-scripts.md)). What you get is the action's state, not the script's.

A pending command can be redelivered until the device acknowledges it. Once an action reaches a terminal failure, it stays failed until you send a new request; Fleet does not automatically retry that failed action.

## Handle collisions, cancellation, and irreversible edge cases

![Troubleshooting](../_assets/icons/troubleshooting-light.svg)

**A locked device must be unlocked before it can be wiped**, on every platform that models locking. Android is the exception, and only because it does not model it.

**Fleet refuses actions that conflict with one already in flight**, and refuses ones whose target is already in the resulting state.

Upcoming work can be cancelled before it is activated ([5.1](5.1-plan-target-and-govern-device-changes.md)). Custom commands have no administrator-facing cancellation, and Fleet cannot recall a command already sent to a device.

An offline Apple device retains its queued instruction and may collect it much later when it reconnects. That MDM queue has no recall operation, so offline status does not provide an additional cancellation window.

**An iOS or iPadOS device that cannot reach Fleet after being locked** cannot be released by Fleet, which is the practical version of the Lost Mode warning above.

**Linux's destructive phase is delayed**, so a Linux wipe reported as successful may still be in progress, and a machine that loses power in that window may be left partially wiped rather than wiped or intact.

## Run the lost-or-stolen-device runbook

![How-to](../_assets/icons/howto-light.svg) During a lost-device incident, use the actions above as part of a coordinated response. The first decision is whether you expect to recover the device or need to erase it; that choice affects the order of operations.

First confirm that the device is enrolled and supports the action you need. An offline device may retain queued work until it reconnects, so you cannot rely on prompt execution. For a device that was never enrolled or whose platform lacks the required action, use the identity, recovery, and incident-response steps at the end of this runbook. Begin those steps while any Fleet action is pending.

| The device is | First move |
|---|---|
| Enrolled and checking in | Work the Fleet steps below |
| Enrolled but offline right now | Queue the lock or wipe (it delivers on the next check-in), and start the non-Fleet handoffs in parallel rather than waiting on it |
| Not enrolled, or enrolled without the device management that lock and wipe need | Fleet has no lane to the device; go straight to the non-Fleet handoffs |
| On a platform Fleet cannot lock or wipe | Use the platform's own find-and-secure service, then the non-Fleet handoffs |

**1. Choose recovery or retirement.** On Apple, Windows, and Linux, [a locked device must be unlocked before Fleet can wipe it](#handle-collisions-cancellation-and-irreversible-edge-cases). That requires the unlock to complete, which may be impossible while the device is unreachable. If your decision is to wipe, proceed to that action without first adding a lock. Android permits wipe while locked because Fleet does not model its lock state.

**2. Contain, matching the move to the decision.** If there is a real chance the device comes back, lock it using [the platform notes above](#lock-unlock-locate-and-clear-passcodes); a locked device is already contained, and on iOS and iPadOS it is in Lost Mode, the only platform where Fleet can then ask for its position. If you are writing the device off, do not lock a modeled-lock platform first: send the wipe directly, or if it is already locked, unlock it and wait for the unlock to land before wiping. Android is the exception either way, because it does not model the lock, so lock-then-wipe works there.

**3. Account for pending work.** A [successful tracked wipe cancels the host's remaining upcoming activities](#wipe-a-device-and-plan-for-what-remains). Its host record and inventory remain available in Fleet; export them separately if your incident process requires a retained snapshot.

**4. Send the wipe when your response calls for it.** Follow the platform notes, including the scope of Android wipe and Linux's path-based deletion. The operation is irreversible once it reaches the device. On Linux, an early success precedes deletion, and loss of power during the destructive phase can leave a partially wiped device.

**5. Verify the outcome.** Check Apple and Windows command results or the tracked device state available on other platforms. The activity feed records the request. For Linux, confirm deletion independently when you can reach or recover the device; waiting out the delay alone does not establish that the wipe completed.

**6. Update the device's lifecycle record.** Decide whether to retain or delete the host in Fleet and record its disposition in your asset system. If it is recovered and re-enrolled, Fleet clears its computed wiped state and resumes the ordinary host lifecycle ([3.1](../03-connect-devices/3.1-enrollment-design-and-host-lifecycle.md)).

### What this runbook hands off

Coordinate the device response with identity, asset management, and your organization's incident process. Fleet's device actions do not revoke the accounts or sessions the device carried. Start these related tasks while the device work proceeds:

- **Identity.** Revoke the user's active sessions and rotate any credential the device held, in your identity provider and in any service that issues its own long-lived tokens. Fleet's login integration ([2.5](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.5-identity-providers-sso-scim-and-role-sync.md)) does not do this for you, and a signed-in session on a stolen laptop outlives the wipe until someone ends it.
- **Physical recovery.** Where Fleet has no locate step, which is everywhere but iOS and iPadOS, the platform's own find service is the only location signal you have. Start it early; it is independent of Fleet and of whether the device ever checks in again.
- **Asset and inventory.** Mark the device lost in whatever system of record owns your hardware, so it stops counting as in service and any warranty or insurance claim has a timestamp.
- **Security, legal, and communications.** Report the loss on whatever path your organization requires. Regulated data on the device, or a theft rather than a misplacement, can carry disclosure or law-enforcement obligations that are decided outside Fleet and on a clock that starts at the loss, not at the wipe.
