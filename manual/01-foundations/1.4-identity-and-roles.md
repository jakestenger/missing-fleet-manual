# Identity and roles

Fleet’s access model helps organizations delegate endpoint work while retaining clear ownership. It combines identity, role, fleet scope, and interface so the people and systems doing the work have the right level of access.

Endpoint management makes access control unusually consequential, because the tool that can read every device is also the tool that can change every device. A mistake in either direction is expensive. Grant too much and a support account can wipe a laptop; grant too little and the people who own a device population cannot do their jobs without escalating to someone who does not know the environment.

## Every action has four access decisions

When someone or something works in Fleet, four decisions combine to determine what can happen.

**Authentication** establishes the identity making the request. For a person, this is normally their Fleet account and organization sign-in. For an automated system, it is a dedicated API-only user or token. Fleet can then attribute the work to a person or system that has a clear owner.

**Role** establishes the kinds of work that identity may perform: observing device state, managing endpoint work, changing configuration, or administering Fleet. A role grants a set of capabilities; it does not by itself decide which devices the identity can reach.

**Scope** establishes where those capabilities apply. A global assignment can work across the organization. A fleet assignment confines the same role to the hosts and fleet-scoped resources in that fleet. One person can have different roles in different fleets when their responsibilities differ.

**Interface** is the way the request reaches Fleet. An operator might use the UI or `fleetctl`. GitOps, a CI job, or an internal workflow might use an API-only identity through GitOps or the REST API. The interface does not bypass identity, role, or scope; it uses the permissions of the identity configured for it.

**The four combine by narrowing, never by widening.** Each one can only reduce what the previous one allowed. That single property is what makes the model safe to reason about: you can check any one of the four in isolation and know that satisfying it is necessary but not sufficient.

For example, a lab employee can sign in through the organization’s identity provider and receive a fleet role for the Computer lab fleet. That assignment lets the employee perform the work their role permits for lab devices, without granting access to a separate Corporate workstations fleet. A central endpoint team can hold the global access needed for organization-wide settings.

## Organizational control vs. ownership of fleets

### Global Admins
Some decisions are naturally organization-wide: server configuration, identity-provider and SCIM setup, MDM connections, audit-log delivery, and other shared settings. Keep these with a small group of global administrators who can coordinate their impact across the deployment.

### fleet roles
Use fleet role assignments for daily endpoint ownership. A fleet owner can be responsible for the policies, software, profiles, and device actions appropriate to their operating environment. This gives a computer lab, business unit, or regional support group meaningful autonomy while preserving clear boundaries between their device populations.

### Role and scope are independent of each other

This is the part of the model people most often collapse. **The same set of roles exists at both levels.** A global maintainer and a fleet maintainer hold the same kind of authority; they differ only in how far it reaches.

That independence is what lets one person hold different roles in different fleets, which is common and correct. Someone can administer the fleet they own while holding read-only access to a neighbouring one they occasionally support.

It also means a role name on its own never answers "what can this person do." It answers half the question. [1.3](1.3-hosts-fleets-labels.md) supplies the other half, and the two compose: **a fleet is simultaneously a boundary around devices and a boundary around people.** That is the single most useful thing to understand about Fleet's access model, and it is why fleets are worth designing deliberately rather than accumulating.

[2.3](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.3-user-accounts-roles-and-service-identities.md) covers which role to choose. [a.4](../09-appendices/a.4-roles-and-permissions-matrix.md) holds the exhaustive breakdown.

### "Read-only" is not the same as "harmless"

Worth pausing on, because the assumption is so natural.

In most systems, read access is the safe thing to hand out. In a device management tool it is not automatically so, because some of what Fleet can read is a secret. At fleet scope, the read-only role can view disk encryption recovery keys.

That is deliberate and useful: recovering a locked-out user is exactly the task you want a support person to handle without also granting them the ability to change configuration. It is simply not what most people picture when they hear read-only.

Treat "who can read recovery secrets" as its own question when you design access, rather than assuming the answer falls out of the role names.

### Fleet also has a write-only role

The mirror image, and just as easy to miss. Fleet's GitOps role can apply configuration but cannot read most of what it applied.

That shape exists for pipelines. A credential living in CI should be able to push desired state and should not become a way to enumerate the estate if it leaks. The practical consequence is that a GitOps token returning nothing when you try to check state is the design working, not a broken token.

## Give people and automation their own identities

Human administrators need an identity that follows their employment and access lifecycle. Connecting Fleet to the organization’s identity provider gives administrators a familiar sign-in experience and makes joiner, mover, and leaver processes easier to operate. SCIM and role-sync features can further align Fleet access with the organization’s established identity processes.

Automation needs the same clarity. Give GitOps, CI, reporting, ticket workflows, and internal tools their own API-only identities rather than sharing a person’s token. Each identity should have an owner, documented purpose, narrowest useful scope, and a rotation and review process. This makes automated changes durable, reviewable, and easier to update when the workflow changes.

`fleetctl` follows the same model. It acts with the identity and scope of its configured session or token, so command-line work remains attributable and respects the same access boundaries.

### Attribution is a reason, not a side effect

Shared credentials do not only blur responsibility. They break the record.

Every action in Fleet is recorded against the identity that performed it, and that record is what [1.5](1.5-audit-and-activity.md) is built on. An action attributed to a person tells you who to ask. An action attributed to a well-named service identity tells you which workflow to inspect. An action attributed to a token three people share tells you almost nothing, and it tells you nothing precisely when you most need to know.

The naming matters as much as the separation. A service identity called `API User` produces an activity record that is technically complete and practically useless six months later.

### Access has a lifecycle, and Fleet should not own it

An account that outlives the person's need for it is the most common access failure in any tool, and it happens by omission rather than decision.

The model's answer is to keep the lifecycle upstream. When Fleet accounts are created and removed from your identity provider, joining, moving, and leaving are handled by a process that already exists and is already audited. Fleet stops holding an independent opinion about who should have access.

That is the destination. Getting there is [2.2](../02-administer-and-deploy-fleet/2.2-identity-providers-sso-scim-and-role-sync.md), which covers sign-in, automatic account creation, role assignment from your directory, and automatic removal.

Automation is the exception that proves the rule. A service identity has no employment lifecycle to inherit, which is exactly why it needs an owner and a review date assigned deliberately.

## Establish a practical access model

Start with a small group of global administrators for shared Fleet configuration. Assign fleet roles to the teams that own day-to-day device work, and use a separate service identity for each automated workflow. Review roles, API tokens, endpoint restrictions, and audit-log delivery on a regular cycle. Treat recovery keys, certificate authorities, secrets, and automation output as sensitive operational data throughout that model.

The full role-and-permission matrix, token administration, GitOps identity setup, and audit-log configuration belong in the reference and operations parts of the manual.
