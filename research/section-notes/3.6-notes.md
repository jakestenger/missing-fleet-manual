## Derived

- **"Scheduled reports do not work"** follows from the extension posting to no logging endpoint. Fleet does not state it; the absence in the source is the evidence, and the claim is phrased as what the extension does rather than as a Fleet limitation.

## Strengthened 2026-08-27, before its review

The first three Part III reviews all came back NOT READY on the chapter's organising claim, so 3.6's was checked against the source. Both halves held: Android really is push-only with no agent, and ChromeOS really does use the osquery channel against its own eleven tables. One capability boundary was missing, and it is the same shape as the one 3.5 was missing.

**Ownership decides whether an Android host can be wiped.** Fleet refuses a wipe on a personally-owned Android host with a message saying to unenroll instead, and a source comment explains why that is not a lesser substitute: unenrolling a work profile runs Google's own wipe of the profile underneath, so routing personal hosts through the wipe flow would be redundant. **Lock works on both** ownership types, with a source comment saying so explicitly, and additionally requires Fleet to consider the host connected.

That makes the path table a lock-and-wipe table too, which is the decision the chapter exists to inform and which cannot be revised without re-enrolling. Sources: `ValidateAndroidWipeRequest` in `server/fleet/hosts.go`, and the `android` branch of the lock path in `ee/server/service/hosts.go`.

## First independent review, 2026-08-27: NOT READY. Applied.

Nine factual defects, three of them high severity, and two of the corrections changed **8.10**.

**"No agent on Android, everything unsolicited" is false, and 1.2, 2.8 and 8.10 all knew it.** Fleet ships a narrow Android app, force-installed with certificate-install delegation, which enrols with Fleet, installs certificates and reports results; and Fleet **polls Google hourly** to reconcile which devices still exist rather than relying only on being told. This is the sharpest instance yet of the pattern the other four chapters showed: the claim is the natural inference from "Android has no fleetd", it was written without checking, and three neighbouring chapters had it right.

**"Absent host means the notification did not arrive" is not a valid diagnosis.** Fleet validates the payload after receipt, requiring hardware, software and memory data, re-verifies the enroll secret embedded in the token, and then does database work. Any of those can fail on a notification that arrived perfectly, and a revoked or rotated secret fails at the second. A `STATUS_REPORT` for an unknown device can also create the host, so `ENROLLMENT` is not the only path in. **8.10 said the same wrong thing in two places** and is corrected with it.

**The enroll secret does not always decide placement, on either platform in this chapter.** On Android re-enrollment Fleet looks up the device's prior team by host key and uses that **in preference to the token's**, even when the host row was deleted in between. On ChromeOS the osquery path does not change `team_id` for a host it recognises, which 3.1 states as a general rule and this chapter had dropped. So a device returning to where it used to be is designed behaviour rather than a wrong secret. **8.10's wrong-team bullet is corrected too.**

**The identity-provider UUID is optional.** Fleet accepts an empty one where authentication is not required and creates the person-to-device association only when it is present, so an enrollment URL circulated without one produces managed devices with no owner recorded. The chapter had said the payload attaches the device "to the right person" flatly, two sentences after correctly calling the UUID optional.

**ChromeOS polls every ten seconds, not every minute.** The main loop reschedules itself on a ten-second timer; the one-minute alarm exists to stop Chrome suspending the service worker. Fleet's own host page reports the interval as ten seconds. The chapter had picked the visible constant over the actual cadence, which is a sixfold error in the latency and load a reader would plan for.

**An unsupported table errors; it does not return nothing.** The extension catches the SQLite error and returns status 1 with the message, so the host shows as failing that report rather than as having nothing to say. Those mean opposite things. Also corrected: not every table is backed by a Chrome *enterprise* API. `disk_info` uses the storage API and **`geolocation` calls an external web service**, which is worth knowing before running it across an estate.

**Three absolutes about failure and inspection.** The ChromeOS failure list named three network and credential causes; the extension can also refuse the platform, fail to initialise its database, or fail the two queries it builds enrollment details from, all before it makes a request. "You cannot go and look at the device" contradicted the chapter's own next paragraph and 8.10's device-side guidance. And the desktop-Chrome refusal is **logged**, not quiet: no Fleet host appears and no user sees anything, but the service-worker console says exactly what happened.

**Marked as Google's rather than asserted:** deleting the enterprise removing every work profile. What the checkout establishes is that Fleet deletes it at Google, drops its own record, turns Android MDM off and marks hosts unenrolled; the device-side consequence is Fleet's guidance.

**Naming reconciled with 1.2:** the ChromeOS extension is *Fleetd for Chrome*, which is its actual name, and the useful statement is that it carries none of the fleetd bundle rather than that it is not fleetd. And "an osquery host on channel 1" became the precise version: the distributed channel is in use, osqueryd is not.

### Omissions from the same review, applied separately

The review's structural point was that the chapter "reads more like an architectural essay than an enrollment chapter", and it was right: neither platform had a procedure an administrator could execute.

**Android now has one.** Open Add hosts, choose Android, pick Personal (BYOD) or Company-owned (fully-managed); Fleet generates a different URL for each and the company-owned one carries `fully_managed=true`, which is what sets `PERSONAL_USAGE_DISALLOWED` on the resulting token. Also worth stating: if the tab tells you to turn Android MDM on instead of showing a URL, the enterprise is not bound and 2.8 is where that happens.

**ChromeOS now has one, and it is the more useful of the two** because nothing else in the manual covers it. Fleet's Add hosts tab supplies three things to paste into Google Admin: the extension ID, the installation URL of Fleet's update manifest, and a policy JSON with `fleet_url` and `enroll_secret` already filled in. Scope them to an organisational unit and set the extension to force install. The chapter now also says the policy JSON contains an enroll secret, which is the security consequence of that flow and is easy to paste past.

**The ChromeOS console is now reachable.** The chapter deferred troubleshooting to "the extension's console" without saying how to open it, which is worse than not mentioning it, and it does so having declared there will be no ChromeOS troubleshooting chapter. It now names the path: `chrome://extensions` with developer mode on, through the service-worker link.

**The Google-account path is out of the decision table.** It was sitting there as a peer choice with "no expected status established" in its cell, which invites a reader to pick it. It is now a note after the two real paths, saying plainly that Fleet's checklist names it and says nothing else, that nothing at this release establishes how it starts or what status it produces, and that it is omitted deliberately rather than overlooked.

### Lock and wipe licensing, added 2026-08-27

Added after 3.5's confirming review found this chapter's licensing wrong in both directions inside one chapter, which suggested checking every licence claim in the part rather than the one that was flagged.

The boundary, from the free service's own implementations: **`LockHost` returns `ErrMissingLicense` unconditionally**, so lock is Premium on every platform. **`WipeHost` refuses any host whose platform is not `android`**, with a source comment saying wipe for macOS, Windows, Linux and iOS/iPadOS is Premium and that Android on Premium goes through the enterprise implementation instead. So **wipe on a company-owned Android host is the one lock-or-wipe operation Fleet Free has**, and it is worth naming, because a Free deployment that needs a remote-wipe story has exactly one place to get it.

## Confirming review, 2026-08-27: NOT READY, applied

Most of the nine first-review findings confirmed resolved, including the Android agent and hourly reconciler, the absent-host diagnosis, the placement rules, the optional IdP UUID, the ten-second cadence, the erroring tables, and both 8.10 corrections. What remained is one pattern, stated by the reviewer better than I would have: **the two high-severity mental-model findings were replaced with unsupported frequency claims.**

**"Almost everything Fleet knows arrives without Fleet asking"** replaced "everything". Both are quantity claims and the source establishes neither: what it establishes is **categories**, and the honest sentence names them. Most of the *traffic* is Google pushing, and Fleet's own app and hourly reconciler are the exceptions, which is what the chapter now says. The comparison table's "whatever Google chooses to push" was the same claim surviving in the place a reader looks first, which is the partial-application shape again.

**Three ChromeOS absolutes had to go, and one of them was a new false statement.** "Repointing the policy at another fleet's secret re-enrols the same Chromebooks where they already are" describes a re-enrollment that does not happen: the extension enrols when it has **no node key**, and re-enrols when a request is refused. Changing a policy value triggers neither. The corrected sentence is more useful anyway, because it says what does not happen and why.

The other two were reach: "no user-visible sign" claims something about Chrome's UI that the platform guard does not establish, and "all six look different" plus "the only troubleshooting surface" plus "one place to look" overstate what the console distinguishes. A failed request can collapse into a generic failure that does not say whether the URL, the certificate or the secret was the problem, and Fleet's own documentation also points at the policy in Google Admin. **There are two places to look, not one**, and the chapter now says so and says why: a value that never reached the extension looks identical from the console to a value it could not use.
