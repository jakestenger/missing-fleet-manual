## Derived

**"Execution, storage and forwarding are three decisions."** Each mechanism is source-checked; presenting them as three independent axes is the chapter's framing, and it is what makes the rest of the chapter usable.

**"A clipped report has unknown coverage and unknown freshness."** Follows from the two cap behaviours: an oversized host stores nothing and the cleanup trims across hosts. This wording was adopted from 1.1's third review, where the reviewer proposed it after retracting a simpler "freeze" description; it is repeated here because it is the safe operational conclusion rather than because the reviewer said it.

**The results-table reading rows.** Individually source-backed; the arrangement into what each appearance is consistent with is editorial.

## Deliberately not stated

**No live-report row or host limit is given.** The tag has a rest period and campaign machinery, but establishing a reader-facing limit needs a check I have not done, and inventing one would be worse than omitting it.

**No claim about how often a clipped report occurs.** The cap is per query across the estate, and the chapter gives the arithmetic (fifty rows per host reaches 1000 at twenty hosts) rather than a frequency.

## Review round, 2026-08-27

NOT READY, seventeen required changes, all applied. The chapter was rewritten rather than patched. Two of the findings were things I had asserted confidently in *two* chapters.

### The governing model was wrong

I opened the draft with "execution, storage and forwarding are three independent decisions" and "none of the three implies another". **Execution is not independent of the other two.** Fleet puts a saved report on a host's schedule only when its interval is positive, its scope matches, **and at least one output path is active**. With storage disabled and automations off, the report is absent from the schedule entirely: it does not run, cost anything, or produce the "empty report page by design" the draft described.

### The cap is two caps, and only one is configurable

The draft said a host result larger than "the cap" stores nothing. There are two limits and they are different numbers:

- a **hard-coded per-host threshold of 1000 rows**, above which that host's rows are all rejected. Fleet receives the configured cap in the same function and does not use it for this decision;
- the configurable **`report_cap`**, default 1000, which drives an across-host cleanup and the clipped marker.

So `report_cap: 2000` still loses a 1001-row host entirely, and `report_cap: 500` inserts an 800-row host and trims it later. A reader raising the cap to retain large per-host results would silently keep losing them. The chapter now gives both cases as worked examples.

### Live reports distinguish more than I credited

I wrote that a live report "cannot tell you the difference" between a host that answered with no rows and one that never answered, and repeated it in the results table. **Fleet counts responders with rows, responders without rows and errors separately, and surfaces per-host error text.** A reader following the draft would have gone to the status logs for information already on the screen. The chapter now has a four-row table making the distinction, and a screenshot brief that captures the counters specifically because they are the part a reader may not know exists.

I also wrote that a live report "stores nothing". Its **result rows** are not retained as host data, but Fleet records an activity for the run and updates the report's performance statistics, so it is auditable.

### Stored empty results are recorded, and which surface you read decides what you see

When a host runs a snapshot report and matches nothing, Fleet stores a marker carrying that host's fetch time. **The estate-wide report excludes those markers**, so the host is simply absent; **the host's own report view includes them with the fetch time**, which is positive evidence the report ran and matched nothing. A scheduled execution failure stores no row of any kind. That is three states and two surfaces, and the draft collapsed them into one table row.

### Names

Three settings and two commands were taught under deprecated spellings. `query_reports_disabled` is now `discard_reports_data`, `query_report_cap` is `report_cap`, `live_query_disabled` is `live_reporting_disabled`, and the canonical commands are `fleetctl report` and `fleetctl get reports`. Corrected here, and **in 1.1**, which carried the old names and the collapsed single-cap description.

### Performance: no trend exists

The draft told readers to watch for execution time "climbing" and memory standing out against neighbours. **Per-host statistics are overwritten on each update rather than kept as a series**, so there is no slope to read, and Fleet's aggregate indicator is computed from current CPU percentiles and execution counts. The method is comparison against other reports on the same hosts, which the chapter now says.

### Added on the completeness question

Saved-but-unscheduled as a first-class mode. An end-to-end workflow from live run to scheduled report. Fleet and label scoping with its Premium gates, and the fact that reports have no exclusion mode. `differential_ignore_removals`. The interface's clipping of very large live results. The destructive effect of editing a saved report's SQL on its stored results. And the forwarding exceptions: when stored reporting is disabled server-wide the automations filter is bypassed, and results for reports Fleet does not recognise are forwarded too, which is also now in **2.5**.

### Boundaries held

Watchdog, denylisting and what to do about an expensive report went to 4.6. Scheduled-report failure diagnosis went to 8.2 and 8.7. Destination configuration stayed in 2.5.
